Chapter 05 · burden → who → why → act

Delhi's heat
has an address.
It's the half of the city
the plan skipped.

Every summer the city discovers it is hot, and blames the planet. Part of that is true. The more local truth is less convenient: Delhi has been built to retain heat, and the hottest rooms were handed to the people it never planned for. On 25 May 2026 the land surface temperature reached 54.61°C — not the air, the heat stored in roads, roofs and walls that radiates back after dark. 75.78% of the city is now persistently heat-stressed. And the choices that built that oven — the master plan, the land, which colonies got parks and which got nothing — were made by an authority the government Delhi elects does not control. The heat is what an unelected city feels like at noon.

1 · What you live with

The city keeps the heat. Some of you keep more.

Land surface temperature is not the number on the weather bulletin. It is the heat the built city stores by day and gives back at night — the heat that makes sleep without a cooler a health risk. On a single May afternoon it ran from 31.59°C to 54.61°C across Delhi, a 23-degree gap between one neighbourhood and another in the same city, on the same day.

Five of CSE's six named hotspots are the atlas's own top five — a single-day satellite snapshot and a multi-year median, two readings, one geography, all on the western and north-western fringe. These are where construction workers, factory hands, street vendors and delivery workers live and work — and they are not where heat deaths are usually counted. 2 · Why it persists

Green cover halved. The water nearly gone.

Concrete and asphalt bank the sun's energy through the day and release it after sunset. Soil, water and trees do the opposite — they cool through shade and evaporation. Remove enough of them and the city loses its night-time recovery, so the next day starts from a hotter base. Over a single decade Delhi did exactly that.

Green cover, 201425.36% of the city
Green cover, 202414.14%  (−44% in ten years)

And the cooling sinks went with the trees. Delhi's waterbody footprint fell from 1.25% to 0.99% of the city in the same decade — ponds and wetlands built over, the evaporation they offered gone with them. This is not an accident of weather. It is the physical form of the city: low-rise density, narrow lanes, little cross-ventilation, vanished water, thin tree cover. The heat has nowhere to go.

3 · Who decided

The hottest wards are the ones the state never planned.

Delhi's housing market pushed migrants and low-income families into unauthorised colonies for decades — neighbourhoods built outside the framework that fixes road width, open space, parks, drainage and tree cover. The city tolerated them because it was cheaper than building legal affordable housing at the scale it needed. The thermal cost is now visible: where there was no room for trees there is no shade; where lanes are too narrow and houses press together, the heat is trapped.

And ask who drew that plan. Delhi's land and its master plan belong to the Delhi Development Authority — a body of the Union government, chaired by the Lieutenant-Governor, that answers to the Centre, not to the government Delhi elects. The city's own elected government does not control its land. So the decisions that set where the parks went, which colonies stayed unplanned, and now which get regularised "as-is" were taken by an office the city's vote does not reach — and regularisation without retrofit simply freezes that into law. The hottest neighbourhoods are the ones an authority the city does not control declined to plan, and is now about to ratify, unchanged.

Delhi's heat is called a climate problem arriving from outside. In the colonies where people suffer most, it has an address, a paper trail — and an authority the city it sits in does not control.

Your money · your mandate

You live in it. They work in it.

For most of Delhi, heat protection is not a clause in any contract. 80% of the city's workforce is informal — and three of every four of its women workers. A worker who stays home in 45°C heat loses the day's wage; a worker who goes out risks collapse. The cost of the city's thermal form is paid first, and hardest, by the people who earn least and choose least about how it is built.

A heat action plan hands out advisories, water points and shelters. Useful for a spell of extreme heat. None of it changes the temperature of a roof, the absence of shade on a street, or the heat trapped in a room at midnight.

4 · The question you're left with

Then who is left to cool the city?

The durable fixes are known — cool roofs in the building rules, tree cover that cannot be waived in the narrowest lanes precisely because that is where it is needed most, enforceable protection for the water bodies, minimum ventilation wherever reconstruction is allowed. Every one of them is a decision of the master plan, the land authority, the building code — and not one of those bodies is on your ballot. That is the real finding under the heat: you cannot vote the oven cool, because you do not yet control the city that built it. The fixes come second. Bringing the city under the people who live in it comes first.

Who makes the durable fixes mandatory?An advisory cools no one at midnight. Cool roofs, lane-level shade, waterbody protection and ventilation norms are building-rule decisions — taken by agencies you do not elect. So who attaches them to the regularisation that is coming anyway, before "as-is, where-is" freezes the heat into law?
Who even counts the dead?Heat deaths are rarely counted in Mundka or Bawana — the wards are not where the toll is tallied, though they are where the exposure is worst. If the hottest neighbourhoods are invisible in the record, what pressure does any office ever feel to cool them?
What body speaks for the worker in the heat?Eighty per cent of Delhi works informally — no contract, no heat clause, no shaded worksite. The resident bodies that claim a neighbourhood answer to flat-owners, not to the delivery rider or the mason out in 45°C. So whose membership card is a day's exposure, and what would a body built from them ask for?
What does your vote even decide here?The city plan, the green cover, the terms of regularisation, the building rules — trace each back and ask which one your councillor, your MLA, or an unelected authority actually controls. One question for every candidate, on the record: what will you do to cool the wards the plan skipped? And if the answer is "that isn't ours" — what is your vote for?

The heat is the symptom. The unelected city is the disease. So the first question isn't how to cool one roof — it is how a city this hot comes to answer to the people who live in it at all. Take the city back, and the rest is reachable. Until then, the oven is someone else's to switch off.
