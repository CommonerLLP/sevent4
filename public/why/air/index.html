Your city measures
its own poison.
Who is left to act on it?

A monitoring station is not a regulator. It records the PM2.5; it cannot close the factory. That job belongs to the State Pollution Control Board — the body that grants permits, inspects industry, and enforces the law. In India's biggest cities, that body is, by its own records, two-thirds empty — and the air keeps its score regardless.

3 · Why it persists

Triple the work. A third more chairs. Most of them empty.

Between 2009-10 and 2020-21 the pollution permits Delhi's regulator had to issue grew far faster than the posts it was given to issue them.

₹85 crore collected and left unspent in a single year. Money was never the bottleneck. The will to staff and spend it was.

And Kolkata is not the exception — it is the rule. Three more cities in this atlas, three more regulators sitting on mountains of money they will not spend. Tamil Nadu's board has banked a ₹2,065 crore surplus and takes nothing from the state — it runs entirely on the consent fees industry pays it, and earns ₹162 crore a year just in interest on the pile. Gujarat's board is larger still: in September 2024 its own board minuted approval to spend down ₹3,230 crore of surplus over five years — money long since collected and sat on, while three of every five of its posts stayed vacant. Karnataka's board opened 2023-24 with ₹1,292 crore in cash, took in ₹349 crore, spent ₹79 crore, and earned ₹67 crore from interest alone.

Four states, four regulators, one habit: take the money industry pays to be regulated, then decline to spend it on the regulating. The surplus is not prudence. It is the ledger of a job left undone — and the air, the water and the workers downwind keep the real account.

Why would a state hollow out its own regulator? Because a pollution board has no voter. A councillor faces an election; a starved bus route loses riders. The board faces no one — so the incentive to staff it never arrives. And asked directly how empty their technical ranks were, Delhi and West Bengal's boards simply did not answer.

The silence is the finding. The air is measured precisely, and acted on by almost no one.

Your money · your mandate

You already paid for this. Twice.

This is not someone else's machine. You fund it and you authorise it — and an empty, hoarding regulator is the return.

Your tax
Your income and taxes fund the state budget — which barely funds the board. WBPCB drew just ₹3.9 cr in state grants against the ₹154 cr it raised itself; in most states, CPR found, the government gives its board nothing.
Your bills
You needn't pay income tax to fund this. The GST on everything you buy — and the consent fees industry passes into prices (₹60 cr in Kolkata alone) — are the board's real income, paid hardest by those who earn least.
Your vote
It authorises the whole chain — the ward councillor, the MLA, the minister, the board they run. You chose the people it all answers to.

You pay in tax for a regulator that doesn't regulate, and again — in your lungs — for the pollution it waves through. And ₹2,893 crore of that money sits idle in fixed deposits.

4 · The question you're left with

Then who is left to make them answer?

The people who breathe the worst air own the least of the city — construction and gig workers out in it all day, street vendors, renters, the young moving town to town for work. They are shut out of the flat-owner bodies that claim to speak for a neighbourhood, and they are exactly the constituency this board never had. None of the questions below ask whether you own anything. They ask who is left to act when the body that decides your air sits on no ballot.

Who even carries the demand?The resident welfare associations that claim to speak for a neighbourhood answer to flat-owners and routinely lock out renters and migrants — the very people in the air all day. So who speaks for the gig worker, the vendor, the tenant, the young and mobile? Their lungs are the only membership card the air recognises — what would a body built from them look like?
What pressure does an office with no voters feel?The board that decides your air faces no election. A protest at its gate, the issue forced into the ward sabha, a feed that won't scroll past — for a generation that organises on a phone, is visibility the only lever an unelected office actually registers?
What would its own record show?Most numbers on this page came back from an RTI any citizen can file for ₹10 — or a single question pressed in Parliament — no deed, no lawyer. Ask the board for its vacancies, budget and inspections. What does it look like when those figures are on a banner instead of buried in a file?
Who uses the levers that already exist — and why so rarely?There is a public objection window when a polluting unit seeks consent, and the National Green Tribunal when the board sits on its hands. A workers' collective or tenants' body filing as a group lands far harder than one name. So why are these doors so seldom walked through?
What is your vote even for here?First-time and migrant voters are concentrated exactly where the air is worst — yet the board that fails them is on no ballot. One question for every candidate, on the record: how many posts in our pollution board sit empty, and what will you do about it? And if the answer is "that isn't ours to fix" — what does your vote decide?
