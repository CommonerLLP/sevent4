Chapter 02 · burden → who → why → act

The same roads,
dug up every year.
By the same few hands.

A road is laid, then laid again, then again. In Ahmedabad you can watch it happen in the city's own ledgers: more than 1,600 resurfacing work-orders across seven years — and a handful of the same firms winning back the same neighbourhoods, year after year. It is not chaos. It is a settled arrangement, and the bill is yours.

1 · The burden

It isn't everyone's road that keeps getting redone

The city keeps a register of every stretch it resurfaces — with the road, the ward, the contractor, and the Defect Liability Period: the years the firm stays on the hook to fix its own bad work. Read seven of those registers together and a pattern falls out that no single year shows. The same favoured wards reappear again and again — while others never appear at all.

Read the two lists back to back. The wards redone six years in seven are not a tidy income map — they run from the affluent western edge (Bodakdev, Gota, Thaltej) to the industrial, working-class north (Naroda, the single most-resurfaced ward of all). What they share is not wealth; it is a contractor who keeps the turf. The five that got nothing are the eastern and peripheral wards — Amraiwadi, Ramol-Hathijan and the rest. That is the first answer to the chapter's question, before any contractor is named: the budget circles the same favoured wards and never reaches the others.

2 · Who

A closed club, and each member's own turf

Ahmedabad has eight million people and no shortage of road-builders. Yet in the years where the register also names the contractor, just seven firms take four out of every five jobs. One firm alone holds nearly a quarter of all the work. This is what concentration looks like on the page:

And the club doesn't compete — it divides.

The finding is not about any one company — it is the shape: a handful of firms, a city carved into stable territories, the same names returning to the same wards while whole eastern wards see no work at all.

3 · Why it persists

Every one of these contracts is approved by the same table

A municipal road contract in Ahmedabad is not awarded by an anonymous process. It is cleared by the corporation's Standing Committee — the small, powerful body that signs off the city's spending. That committee has been controlled by a single party for the whole of this seven-year record, and it chairs every award. When the same body approves every contract and the same firms keep their wards, the absence of churn is not a coincidence; it is the system working as built.

We tested the obvious suspicion — that the firms are tied to the ruling party directly — against open records: electoral-bond filings, and company directors checked against the councillor rolls for both terms. On open data, no such link is establishable, and we make none. What is documentable is structural and sufficient: one party holds the committee, the committee holds the award, and the award keeps returning to the same hands. You do not need a secret to explain a closed market — you only need an award table with no one to answer to.

The defect-liability irony

Here is the part that should sting. Every one of those work-orders carries a Defect Liability Period — the contractor is legally bound to repair, at its own cost, any road that fails within a few years of laying it. A system built around defect-liability is supposed to make roads last. Instead the same wards are resurfaced six years in seven. Either the roads keep failing inside their liability window and are quietly redone as fresh paid work, or the liability is never enforced. Both readings point the same way: the clause that should end the annual digging is the one no one collects on.

And the corporation keeps the receipt. Its budget books carry road resurfacing as a standing capital head, funded every single year — and, on the income side, a line named in the city's own ledger: “Penalty Recovered from Contractors.” The money the AMC is meant to claw back from firms whose roads fail has its own budget code (23306), printed in book after book for nearly two decades. The defect-liability isn't an abstraction the city forgot — it is a number the city prints a slot for. The only question the chapter can't answer from outside is what goes in that slot. That one is an RTI away.

A road that lasts is a contract that ends. A road redone every year is a relationship that never has to.

Your money · your mandate

You paid for every one of these layers

Road resurfacing is funded straight from the municipal budget — your property tax, your user charges, the grants your taxes send the city. A road relaid before it needed to be is not a free road; it is one you bought twice. And unlike the air or the water, this is a service the elected corporation fully controls — there is no state board to blame, no parastatal in the way. The councillor you vote for sits in the house that owns this. Which makes it the rare failure your vote can actually reach — if the contract were ever made to answer to it.

4 · The question you're left with

So who makes the award answer?

The people who live with the worst roads — the eastern wards that got no resurfacing at all — are the least able to force a re-tender. None of the questions below need a lawyer or a leak. They need the city's own register, read out loud.

Why this ward and not mine?The resurfacing register is a public document, ward by ward. Pull your ward's seven-year row against Naroda's or Bodakdev's. When a residents' body or a ward-sabha puts the two lists side by side, the question stops being "where are our roads" and becomes "why does the budget circle the same favoured wards and never reach ours?"
Who is on the hook — and was the liability ever called?Every stretch names a Defect Liability Period and the firm bound by it, and the budget already has the line: “Penalty Recovered from Contractors” (head 23306). Ask the corporation, by RTI for ₹10: what was actually credited to that head each year, and how many defect-liability claims were enforced? A number near zero is the finding.
Why does the same firm keep the same ward?Tender records, bidder lists and the Standing Committee's minutes are obtainable. Who else bid for your ward's work — and lost, every year? A market with one perennial winner is a question the awarding committee can be made to answer in the open.
What is your vote even for here?This is the one failure with no parastatal to hide behind — the elected corporation awards these contracts itself. One question for every councillor and candidate, on the record: how many firms won our ward's road work in the last seven years, and will you publish the tender file? If the answer is silence, the silence is the platform.
