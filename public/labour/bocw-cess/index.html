Findings · Construction-worker cess

Where does the construction-worker cess actually go?

Every construction project in India pays a 1% cess on its cost into a state welfare board built for one purpose: to fund the workers who build it — safety kits, maternity benefit, school scholarships, medical aid. Nine state boards' own CAG performance audits (2021–2025) answer two separate questions: how much of that money reached anyone, and how many registered workers the board can even confirm still exist. Read city by city, or read it as one pattern.

A 1% tax that funds itself

The Building & Other Construction Workers (Regulation of Employment & Conditions of Service) Act, 1996 and its companion Cess Act require every employer to pay 1% of construction cost to a state Welfare Board, which registers workers and disburses benefits. Nationally the pattern is already visible at the top: the Ministry of Labour told Parliament the unspent balance rose from ₹27,881 cr (30-09-2018) to ₹65,967 cr (31-03-2024) — collection consistently outruns spending, and the gap compounds every year.1

Gujarat's cess never even reaches its own Board.

Of the nine states, Gujarat is the only one where the state government itself — not the welfare board — holds most of the money. No Welfare Fund was ever constituted, so cess sits in the Government Account by default. Only 53% of what was deposited was ever released to the Board at all. That is a distinct failure from "the board hoards it" — here the money doesn't even get that far.

Karnataka is the one state whose audit doesn't fit the pattern.

Spend improved sharply from 2021-22 onward (two scheme payouts of 1,997 cr and 2,076 cr), enough that on a cumulative since-inception basis it is the healthiest board of the nine — 49.2% idle against 63–83% for the other eight ranked states. That figure is a primary Rajya Sabha reply (see below), not a secondary lead: the pattern isn't structural to how BOCW boards work everywhere; something changed in Karnataka that didn't change in the other eight.

How many workers the boards can even confirm they have

This is a harder question than it sounds, because the nine audits don't measure the same thing. Some report an active-registration renewal rate; others report coverage of the eligible workforce; others report scheme uptake; two report on how much of their own registration data they can actually verify. Forcing all nine into a single percentage would misrepresent what the reports say — so here is each state's own registration finding, honestly labelled.

The more defensible finding isn't any single ratio — it's that six boards can't trust their own numbers.

At least six of the nine states' own audits — Karnataka, Delhi, Maharashtra, Uttar Pradesh, Gujarat, and (for establishment registration specifically) West Bengal — flag their own registration data as containing duplicate entries, no unique worker ID, self-certification accepted without verification, or unreconciled parallel databases. That is a stronger, plainer claim than any percentage above: most of these welfare boards cannot currently say, with data they trust themselves, how many workers they actually serve.

So the question isn't "which state spends the least" or "which ratio is worst." A cess exists because construction work is dangerous, informal and cash-in-hand — the fund is supposed to be the one place a bricklayer or a scaffolder can turn for a safety net paid for out of the very project that employs them. If six of nine boards can't say how many workers are actually on their books, and eight of nine have spent less than two-fifths of what came in, what is the cess fund currently doing instead of that job — and for whom?
