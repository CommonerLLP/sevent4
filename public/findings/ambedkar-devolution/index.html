Findings · Ambedkar on devolution

Should your city have more power?

The rest of this atlas shows your elected city running a fraction of itself — the water, the buses, the metro, the land lifted into boards no resident votes for. The obvious conclusion is give the city more power. But the man who drafted the Constitution would say that is the wrong question — and his reasons are uncomfortably relevant.

He had no ideology of centralise or decentralise

Ambedkar judged every tier of government by one test: not "is power local?" but can the people at the bottom be captured at this scale? Put a power where it can be competently run, honestly financed, and cannot become an instrument of social domination. From that single test, his seemingly contradictory positions all follow.

A strong centre — as a shield

In his 1939 R.R. Kale Memorial Lecture at the Gokhale Institute, published as Federation versus Freedom, he was blunt:

"I am not opposed to a Federal Form of Government. I confess I have a partiality for a Unitary form of Government. I think India needs it." B.R. Ambedkar, Kale Memorial Lecture, 1939 (Writings and Speeches, Vol. 1, p.353)

The title is the argument: a federation built on the 1935 Act's terms — locking the autocratic princely states into the constitution — would buy "federation" at the cost of freedom, by entrenching unaccountable power. He carried the point into the Constituent Assembly in 1948:

"Federalism is described as a weak if not an effete form of Government… Such a power of converting itself into a unitary State no federation possesses." B.R. Ambedkar, introducing the Draft Constitution, 4 November 1948

So he gave the Union residuary powers, an emergency switch to unitary rule, and no right of exit ("no State has the right to secede"). Not statism for its own sake — a centre strong enough to guarantee rights uniformly and shield the minority from majoritarian capture below. (Tillin 2021; Chakrabarty 2006.)

Against the village — the part that should unsettle

If devolution is good because it is "local," the village is its purest form. Ambedkar despised it:

"What is the village but a sink of localism, a den of ignorance, narrow-mindedness and communalism?" B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly, 1948

The smaller and more intimate the unit, the tighter the dominant caste's grip and the less the law reaches: in the city, he held, the government could enforce law fairly; in the village the legal mechanism had "no force" against social discrimination (Cháirez-Garza 2014). He was, as Corbridge puts it, "a supporter of centralised parliamentary government rather than of village autonomy." Devolving power down can simply hand the oppressed to their oppressors.

But smaller states — and the self-governing city

He was not, then, a pure centraliser. At the provincial tier he wanted more, smaller states — "one language, many states" — because homogeneity made democracy work and large states concentrated dominant-group power. And he proposed that Bombay govern itself as a city-state (see the Bombay finding). The city, unlike the village, was where caste loosened and law could be impartial — so self-government was safer at city scale than at village scale. Scale by function, not slogan.

And honest money

His first scholarship, in New York and London, was literally on fiscal decentralisation — his Columbia thesis was subtitled A Study in the Provincial Decentralization of Imperial Finance. The test he carried from it: a unit should "finance [itself] entirely out of [its] own respective resources without having to depend upon one another." (Sridevi 2020.) Power without own-revenue is hollow — which is exactly the trap of a "devolved" Indian city that cannot tax.

So, should your city have more power? Ambedkar's answer is to replace the question. Not "is power local?" but: power to do what, accountable to whom, financed how — and could this tier become an instrument of exclusion? A strong, rights-guaranteeing centre plus empowered, well-financed, rightly-scaled units. Never the romantic village; never devolution as a slogan.

His own evidence is the warning. Ahmedabad is one of India's more devolved, better-funded corporations — and it administers the Disturbed Areas Act and lets Juhapura go short on water and schools. That is devolution that became an instrument of exclusion: his nightmare case, not his ideal.

And the question his answer leaves open, the one this atlas keeps: the centre he built to break princely autocracy is the same centre that today suspends elected states and dissolves city councils. Can you have the centre that guarantees rights without the centre that hollows out the city?
