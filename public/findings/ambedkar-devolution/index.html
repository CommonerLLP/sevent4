Findings · Ambedkar on devolution

Where is power safe?

The rest of this atlas shows your elected city running a fraction of itself. The reflex is give the city more power. Ambedkar's reflex was a question — and not the one you'd expect. Not is power local or central? but: can the people at the bottom be captured at this scale? Run his test down the tiers and the answer refuses to line up.

Read left to right and the line refuses to be a gradient. Power is not safer for being local — the village is the worst place of all — nor for being central — the centre is a shield and a weapon at once. It is safe only where it cannot be captured and the law still reaches. That, not "decentralise," is Ambedkar's actual test.

A strong centre — as a shield, not a creed

In his 1939 R.R. Kale Memorial Lecture, published as Federation versus Freedom, he was blunt: "I confess I have a partiality for a Unitary form of Government. I think India needs it." The title carries the argument — a federation on the 1935 Act's terms, locking the autocratic princely states into the constitution, would buy "federation" at the cost of freedom. In the Assembly in 1948 he put it sharper:

"Federalism is described as a weak if not an effete form of Government… Such a power of converting itself into a unitary State no federation possesses." B.R. Ambedkar, introducing the Draft Constitution, 4 November 1948

Residuary powers to the Union, an emergency switch to unitary rule, no right to secede. Not statism — a centre strong enough to guarantee rights uniformly and shield the minority from capture below. (Tillin 2021; Chakrabarty 2006.)

But never the village — and yes the city

If "local" were the test, the village would pass best. It fails worst: the law has no force where the dominant caste rules absolutely, so devolving power down can simply hand the oppressed to their oppressors (Cháirez-Garza 2014; Corbridge 2000). The city is the opposite — caste loosens, law can be impartial — which is why he could imagine Bombay governing itself as a city-state (the Bombay finding), and why he wanted the province broken into many small states rather than few big blocs.

And honest money

His first scholarship, in New York and London, was literally on fiscal decentralisation — his Columbia thesis was subtitled A Study in the Provincial Decentralization of Imperial Finance. The rule he carried: a unit should "finance [itself] entirely out of [its] own respective resources without having to depend upon one another" (Sridevi 2020). A "devolved" city that cannot tax is hollow by design.

So — should your city have more power? Ambedkar replaces the question. Not "is power local?" but: power to do what, accountable to whom, financed how — and could this tier become an instrument of exclusion?

His own warning is on this atlas. Ahmedabad is one of India's more devolved, better-funded corporations — and it administers the Disturbed Areas Act and lets Juhapura go short on water and schools. Devolution that became exclusion: his nightmare, not his ideal.

And the question his answer leaves open — the one this atlas keeps: the centre he built to break princely autocracy is the same centre that today dissolves elected councils. Can you have the centre that guarantees rights without the centre that hollows out the city?
