Findings · a city as a polity

Should a city be its own state?

In 1955, working out how India should be carved into linguistic states, B.R. Ambedkar made a proposal almost no one remembers: Bombay should belong to neither Maharashtra nor Gujarat. It should be its own state. It is the closest thing in Indian political thought to a positive theory of the city as a self-governing polity — which is exactly why it is worth re-reading now.

The proposal

The fight over the 1956 reorganisation was, on the surface, about language. But the real prize was the city. Maharashtra wanted Bombay; the Mahagujarat movement and much of the city's (largely Gujarati-speaking) commercial elite wanted it kept out — ideally as a separate "city-state" of the Union. (Sud & Tambs-Lyche, 2011.) Ambedkar's answer cut across both:

"I have said that Bombay be given a new area and made into a separate City State… I have said that Maharashtra has always been divided into three." B.R. Ambedkar, Thoughts on Linguistic States (1955), in Writings and Speeches (Moon ed.)

It followed from a principle he held against the grain of the whole reorganisation: one state, one language — but not one language, one state. A single language, he argued, could and should "divide [itself] into many States." A great city did not have to dissolve into the linguistic region around it; it could be governed as itself.

Who really wanted Bombay out — and why

Ambedkar refused the linguistic framing and read the dispute as a question of class. The demand to keep Bombay out of a Marathi-majority Maharashtra, he noted, was a demand to protect the city's owners from its workers:

"If the owners and capitalists of Bombay are to be protected by the exclusion of Bombay from Maharashtra because Maharashtrians belong to the working classes, what is the method they suggest for protecting the capitalists of Gujarat from the working classes?" B.R. Ambedkar, Thoughts on Linguistic States (1955)

And he saw the same logic in the States Reorganisation Commission's compromise — a "mixed", bilingual Bombay state — reading it not as neutrality but as a device:

"The Commission in retaining the mixed State of Bombay had to secure two most important objects. One is not to allow Bombay to go into the hands of Maharashtrians." B.R. Ambedkar, Thoughts on Linguistic States (1955)

Why it still matters

History went the other way: after the Samyukta Maharashtra agitation, Bombay became the capital of Maharashtra in 1960. Ambedkar's city-state never happened. But the idea is the missing positive term in this atlas. Everywhere else we document the city being hollowed out — its water, buses, metro and land lifted into state boards no resident elects. Here, uniquely, is the opposite move: the city claimed as a government in its own right, at a scale that could actually answer for itself.

So the question this finding poses is not "was Ambedkar right about Bombay." It is: if a metropolis is too large, too distinct, and too economically dominant to be governed well from a state capital that does not depend on it — what should govern it instead?

And the harder follow-on, the one this atlas keeps returning to: a self-governing city would hold more power. Power to do what, and for whom? A city-state is not automatically a more accountable city — it is only a different scale at which the same questions of who decides, who profits, and who is shut out come due.
