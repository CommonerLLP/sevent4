Findings · Ambedkar on the city

Why did Ambedkar trust the city?

This atlas leans toward the city — the scale where, it keeps arguing, caste loosens and the law can be impartial. But why take a Dalit thinker's word that the city is safer? Because he did not reason it. He lived it. The same man — a Columbia doctorate, an officer of a princely state — was a different person at different scales, and the variable was always one question: could caste still reach him here?

One man, three scales, one decade. The bigger and more anonymous the place, the less of him caste could hold. Not because the city was just — but because it was too large for any one group to own. Anonymity, not justice, was the shield. That is the lived root of the abstract test in the sister finding: not is power local? but can the people at the bottom be captured here?

The bottom: where rank meant nothing

In 1917 Ambedkar came back from Columbia and the London School of Economics to take up a post with the Gaekwad of Baroda — a Maharaja who had funded his studies. He arrived an officer of the state and could not find a roof. No caste-Hindu house would take a Mahar; he talked his way into a Parsi inn under an assumed name. It held ten days.

"Where was he, a Mahar, to stay? He persuaded a Parsi innkeeper to board and lodge him… after ten days a number of Parsis armed with lathis called on him." B.R. Ambedkar, Waiting for a Visa, in Writings and Speeches (Moon ed.)

Given till evening to go, he sat in a public garden and left the town. At Borsad, posted to the Mamlatdar's office, the same machinery ran on water: a peon doled it into a rusty pot kept apart for him, and on the days the peon slipped away he simply went thirsty. The lesson he drew was not about cruelty. It was about scale: in a place small enough that everyone knew who you were, a doctorate, a salary and the Maharaja's seal were worth nothing. The caste order reached all the way to the top of your achievements and switched them off.

The top: where he was first a person

Set that against New York, four years earlier. At Columbia he sat in John Dewey's lectures — a debt he never stopped naming. As one of the first scholars of the connection put it, "unless we understand something of John Dewey… it would be impossible to understand Dr. Ambedkar" (Kadam, in Stroud 2023). What the metropolis gave him was not only Dewey's philosophy but the plain experience of being unmarked — a student among students, placed by his arguments and not his birth. He carried Dewey's definition of democracy home almost verbatim:

"Democracy is not merely a form of Government. It is primarily a mode of associated living, of conjoint communicated experience." B.R. Ambedkar, paraphrasing Dewey, Annihilation of Caste (1936)

Associated living is exactly what the village forbids and the great city, at its best, allows: strangers mixing on terms not fixed in advance by birth. The city was attractive to him for a structural reason, not a sentimental one — it was the one scale large enough to dilute the group's grip on the individual.

But the city was never innocent

This is where the honesty has to come in, because the atlas's own maps forbid the easy version. The city loosened caste; it did not abolish it. Bombay had its segregated chawls and its separate wells; the metropolis sorted by community even as it freed the individual to move. Ambedkar trusted the city as the safer scale, never the just one — and he warned, repeatedly, that a strong local power could itself become the instrument of exclusion rather than the escape from it.

That warning is live on this very atlas. Ahmedabad — one of India's better-funded, more devolved corporations — is also the city that administers the Disturbed Areas Act, formalising the line between Hindu and Muslim neighbourhoods, while Juhapura goes short on water and schools. A city given more power used that power to sort its people. (the devolution finding.) The scale that freed Ambedkar can be re-engineered to do the opposite.

So the question this finding poses is not "did Ambedkar love cities." It is sharper, and it points at us. He escaped capture by moving up the scale — village to town to metropolis to the foreign city — until he was anonymous enough to be free. The shield was bigness; the threat was anyone who could see all of you at once.

And today's city is being quietly rebuilt to see all of you again — sorted by religion through the Disturbed Areas Act, by ownership through the bodies that lock out renters and migrants, by a core the residents cannot even elect. If anonymity was what made the city safe, what happens when the city is re-made to know exactly who you are?
