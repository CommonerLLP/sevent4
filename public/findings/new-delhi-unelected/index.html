Findings · power & the ballot

Who elects the government of New Delhi?

The square miles that hold Parliament, every central ministry, the Prime Minister's residence and the most expensive addresses in India are run by a municipality of their own — the New Delhi Municipal Council. Not one member of that council is directly elected to it by the people who live there. By statute. The richest enclave of the capital is also the least democratically governed — and that is the design, not an accident.

Two Delhis, two kinds of government

Delhi's municipal map is split. The vast majority of the city — about 11 million people across 1,397 km² (2011 Census) — is governed by the Municipal Corporation of Delhi, whose 250 ward councillors are directly elected. A small, dense core — Lutyens' Delhi, the secretariat, Connaught Place, the diplomatic enclave — is carved out and handed to the NDMC, whose councillors are not elected at all.

Who actually sits on the council

The New Delhi Municipal Council Act, 1994 (Section 4) sets out exactly who governs New Delhi — a council of thirteen members: a chairperson, a central-government officer of Joint-Secretary rank or above, appointed by the Centre in consultation with Delhi's Chief Minister; two members of the Delhi Legislative Assembly whose constituencies touch the area; five officials of the Union or Delhi government nominated by the Centre; four more members nominated by the Centre in consultation with the Chief Minister; and the Member of Parliament for New Delhi. The vice-chairperson is chosen from among these members, not separately elected. (The Assembly seats were cut from three to two by amendment in 2012.)

Read the list again for the one thing it never contains: a single member directly elected to the council by the people of New Delhi. The only elected figures on it — the two MLAs and the MP — were elected to the Delhi Assembly and to Parliament, sit ex officio, and represent areas only partly inside the NDMC. The residents of the capital's core have a vote for Parliament and for the Assembly; for their own municipal government, they have none.

And it is not a poor relation

This is no neglected backwater being run from above for want of capacity. By its own 2025-26 estimates the NDMC expects to run a surplus of ₹463 crore — receipts of ₹5,771 crore against ₹5,307 crore of spending. It is one of the very few municipal bodies in India that is structurally in the black, governing the residents who least need the state to economise on their behalf.

A caution this atlas owes you: the two budgets are not a clean per-resident comparison. Roughly a third of the NDMC's money runs through its own electricity-distribution business — a buy-and-sell pass-through the MCD does not carry — and the New Delhi area's daytime population of office-workers and visitors dwarfs its 258,000 residents. So do not read the totals as “rupees spent per head.” The point that survives every caveat is not the size of the budget. It is whose signature authorises it, and that no resident's ballot is anywhere in that chain.

The unelected city, in its purest form

Everywhere else in this atlas the city is hollowed out by degrees — its water, buses, land and air lifted into state boards and parastatals no one elects, until the ward you do vote for controls almost nothing. New Delhi is the same logic taken to its limit and made constitutional: not a function quietly removed, but the entire municipal government placed beyond the vote — and placed there precisely where the country's wealth and power are densest.

So the question this finding poses is not whether New Delhi is well run. By the ledger, it is run well and richly. The question is the one the atlas keeps arriving at from a new direction: when the best-resourced local government in the country is also the one its residents cannot elect, what is the thing we actually value in a city — that it be administered well, or that it answer to the people who live in it?

And the follow-on that should unsettle anyone who would simply “give cities more power”: here is a city with enormous municipal power and a permanent surplus, and none of it is accountable at the ballot. Power devolved is not the same as power answerable.
