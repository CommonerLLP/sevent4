# The frozen base: property tax in six Indian cities

Property tax is the one major tax an elected Indian city owns outright — locally set,
locally collected, and broadly progressive, since it falls on the value of immovable
wealth. It is also chronically starved. The RBI's *Report on Municipal Finances*
(November 2024) gives the anchor numbers (Tier A): municipal revenue receipts are just
**0.6% of GDP** against 9.2% for the Centre and 14.6% for the states; own tax revenue is
only **~30%** of total municipal revenue, the rest being state and central transfers.
Within own taxes, property tax is dominant — **>16% of total** corporation revenue and
**>60% of own-tax** revenue — yet it amounts to only **0.15% of GDP**, versus 1.7% in
advanced economies. The tax that could fund the city is collected at roughly a tenth of
its potential intensity. This is the fiscal substance of the "74th-Amendment-without-
devolution" critique: cities are handed a progressive instrument and then prevented,
politically, from using it.

## The assessment-system split

Three families divide the six. **ARV / Annual Rental Value** — the colonial,
notional-rent base, the most manipulable — survives in **Chennai** (Madras City
Municipal Corporation Act 1919) and **Hyderabad** (monthly-rental-value slabs). **Capital
Value System** (market/ready-reckoner value) is **Mumbai's**, adopted 2010 and litigated
ever since. **Area / unit-area** formulae — area × location/use factors × rate, which
strip out assessor discretion — govern **Bengaluru** (Unit Area Value with
self-assessment, 2000/2008), **Kolkata** (Unit Area Assessment, legislated 2016/17 after
~150 years of colonial ARV), and **Ahmedabad** (carpet-area based since 2001). The split
matters: ARV cities are structurally the most under-assessed, because "fair rent" is
frozen and any revision is electorally radioactive; area-based cities self-adjust better
but still leak through coverage gaps.

## Who collects best, who collects worst

**Ahmedabad collects best.** The 2001 shift to a GIS-driven, area-based system removed the
discretion that had enabled under-assessment; AMC took ~Rs 1,680 cr in FY2023-24, backed
by aggressive sealing drives and an advance-payment rebate. **Kolkata has historically
collected worst** — the 150-year ARV legacy, a slow 2017-22 UAA rollout, and a deep
arrears stock it is only now clawing back (~Rs 1,258 cr in FY24-25, on the back of an
arrears-amnesty that itself confesses the backlog). Between them: **Bengaluru** is the
cautionary tale — self-assessment, sold as anti-corruption, produced systemic
under-declaration (collection efficiency fell to 68% by 2017-18; the 2024-25 e-Khata audit
found ~Rs 390 cr of evasion across ~5 lakh properties, one in seven buildings
under-reporting; rates went unrevised from 2000 against a 3-yearly mandate). **Hyderabad**
collects a rising ~Rs 1,917 cr but sits on ~Rs 5,500 cr of arrears, ~Rs 4,400 cr of it
owed by *government departments* — the state will not pay the tax it sets. **Mumbai** takes
the largest single sum (~Rs 4,300 cr) but has frozen its own base: the up-to-500-sq.ft
exemption (now 700) costs ~Rs 462 cr a year, and postponing the mandatory five-yearly
capital-value revision cost another ~Rs 1,080 cr. **Chennai** sat on a 1998 valuation for
two decades; a 2013 revision order was halted in 2019.

## Own-revenue dependence and the politics of the frozen base

The common mechanism is blocked revaluation. Cooper's reading of New York's fiscal crisis
names it precisely: "property assessors regularly underestimated the market value of real
estate," so the relative burden on owners "dwindled" even as the nominal tax stood. The
Indian cities reproduce this — exemptions for electoral constituencies (Mumbai's flats),
deferred general revisions (Chennai, Hyderabad, Bengaluru), and defaulting public bodies
(Hyderabad). And as the progressive tax is held down, the regressive substitutes rise:
Cooper's "nakedly regressive types of collateral such as user fees" — water charges,
development fees, conservancy levies billed often by unelected state boards (see
`user_fees.json`). The burden shifts from the asset to the user.

## The colonial inheritance

None of this is new. Ghosh (2020) shows property/house tax *was* the original municipal
base: 19th-century Calcutta's services were "paid off from the revenue generated from
house taxes," and house tax shot up "from 5% to 7½%" by 1856. The injustice was not the
tax but its incidence — "devolution itself was a travesty," higher and higher taxes
"without any hope of real representation," while the commercial classes remained "the most
lightly taxed people imaginable in Calcutta." Native householders carried **59.5%** of
Calcutta's rates and taxes while the Bengal Chamber of Commerce resolutely refused to be
taxed. The pattern — householders bearing the load, capital lightly touched, the elected
voice absent — is inherited, not invented. The frozen base is a colonial bequest.
