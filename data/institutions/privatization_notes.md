# The unelected city, privatized: who runs what you cannot vote out

The atlas's earlier maps showed the elected corporation hollowed by *parastatals* — boards
and SPVs that lift water, land and transit out of the council's reach. This note tracks the
harder case: services run not by an unelected *state* body but by an unelected *private firm*.
A water board is at least publicly owned and, however thinly, answerable to the government you
elect. A private monopoly answers to its shareholders and a sector regulator. The citizen is
no longer even a thin democratic principal; she is a **captive consumer**. You cannot vote out
Torrent, Adani, CESC or Tata Power.

## The pattern: electricity is the colonial private carve-out

Four of our six major cities have privately-distributed electricity at the city level, and
the lineage is older than independence. **Kolkata's CESC** began in 1899 as the Calcutta
Electric Supply Corporation — private from inception, never a state discom — and today is the
sole distributor across the 567 sq km of the Kolkata Municipal Corporation, ~4.7M customers,
now the flagship of the RP-Sanjiv Goenka Group (CESC, Wikipedia; RPSG). **Ahmedabad's Torrent
Power** descends from the Ahmedabad Electricity Company (incorporated 1915); Torrent bought out
the Gujarat government's residual stake in 1997 and runs all distribution in the city of
~4.2M-plus customers across its licence areas (Torrent Group history; BEE audit FY20-21).
These are not post-liberalization sell-offs but **colonial-era private licensees that simply
never left** — the same Calcutta/Bombay corporate-municipal lineage Ghosh traces, where civic
provisioning was a profit-bearing enterprise from birth.

The newer mechanisms layer on top. **Mumbai's suburbs** run on private power via a chain of
acquisitions: BSES → Reliance Infrastructure → **Adani Electricity Mumbai (AEML)**, which
bought Reliance Infra's integrated Mumbai business for ~Rs 18,800 crore in August 2018 — over
3 million customers across 400 sq km (Business Standard, 29 Aug 2018; Adani Energy Solutions).
Tata Power holds a parallel Mumbai licence (~0.8M consumers). **Bhubaneswar** shows the
Electricity-Act-2003 template directly: the state utility CESU was privatized in 2020, Tata
Power taking a 51% controlling stake and a 25-year licence as **TPCODL** via the OERC vesting
order of 26 May 2020 — ~3.08M customers, ~13.6M people (Mercom India; Wikipedia TPCODL).

Across these, roughly **35-40 million people** draw privately-distributed electricity (the
discom licence areas run wider than the core city). Their only recourse is a State Electricity
Regulatory Commission — GERC, MERC, WBERC, OERC — a tariff tribunal, not a ballot.

## The genuinely municipal case — and a de-privatization

Two entries cut against the easy story, and both must be stated honestly.

First, **electricity distribution is not a 12th-Schedule municipal function** — the Schedule
covers water, sanitation, roads, health, planning, not power. So privatized electricity is
*not* a devolution failure in the strict 74th-Amendment sense. The genuinely municipal
privatization in this map is **Nagpur water**: a core 12th-Schedule function handed under a
25-year PPP O&M concession to **Orange City Water** (a 50:50 Vishvaraj-Veolia JV) — five plants,
~675 MLD, ~2,100 km of network, the taps of ~2.5M people, contested locally over non-revenue-
water and coverage (Veolia India; SANDRP). That is the sharpest case: not a peripheral utility
but the drinking water of a million-plus city, run by a French multinational's JV.

Second, **Hyderabad Metro** has just moved the *other* way. Built as the world's largest metro
PPP under an L&T SPV (operations from 2017), it was **acquired by the Telangana government in
April 2026** for ~Rs 1,461 crore (Deccan Chronicle; Prokerala). It is now state-owned — a rare
**re-municipalization** worth flagging precisely because it shows privatization is not
one-directional. (service_providers.json should be updated accordingly.)

## The Adani concentration: the unelected city at maximum density

The privatization map's deepest risk is not many small carve-outs but **one conglomerate
accumulating across the whole infrastructural stack**. The Adani Group now runs: suburban
Mumbai's electricity (~12M people); India's largest private airport portfolio — **both** of
Mumbai's airports (CSMIA from GVK in 2021; Navi Mumbai, opened Dec 2025) and Ahmedabad's SVPI
(50-year lease from 2020), ~25% of national passenger footfall, ~33% of air cargo (AAHL);
India's largest port operator (APSEZ, ~27% cargo share); and, with Google, India's largest
data-centre campus at Visakhapatnam on SEZ land held by APIIC — entirely outside the elected
GVMC (Adani/Google release, 2026). Electricity, airports, ports, data, SEZ land: chokepoints of
urban life in multiple atlas cities, each regulated piecemeal (MERC, AERA, OERC, state industry
departments) and accountable to no electorate.

This is where Cooper sharpens the frame. The privatization of public goods is not a neutral
efficiency move but a **counterrevolution** — a deliberate withdrawal of the democratic claim
on the economy, refitting what was once a public, contestable good as a private asset whose
returns flow to shareholders and whose terms are set by regulators insulated from the vote. And
it rhymes with Ghosh's colonial municipality, which offloaded its costly functions while
keeping the rent: here the state offloads the *capital-intensive provisioning* to private
monopolies, retaining the broom (roads, drains, waste) for the elected corporation. The citizen
keeps her vote for the potholes and loses it for the power, the airport, the water, the land.
That is the unelected city's final form — **provisioning without representation.**
