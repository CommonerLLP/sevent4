# Chennai: the oldest corporation in India, and one of the most hollowed

On 29 September 1688 the East India Company chartered the Corporation of Madras — the **oldest municipal corporation in India**, older than Calcutta's, Bombay's, and every other on this atlas by roughly two centuries. Three and a third centuries later, that same body, now the Greater Chennai Corporation, is among the **most hollowed** corporations we map. This is the central irony of the unelected city: age did not protect devolution. The 74th Amendment (1992) promised that an *elected* corporation would run your water, your planning, your housing, your transport. In Chennai almost none of it does. And the decision to take each function away was made, every single time, by the **state government at Fort St George or by the Centre — never once by the elected council**.

Read the carve-outs in order and a pattern hardens. Unusually, Madras had **no classic colonial Improvement Trust** — the depoliticising body Anwesha Ghosh anatomises for Calcutta and Bombay. The hollowing here is therefore not a colonial inheritance but a **post-Independence state project**, built deliberately, decade by decade.

**Housing first (1961).** The Tamil Nadu Housing Board (TN Act 17 of 1961) took land assembly and housing development — the rent-rich function of deciding who gets housed where — into a state agency before the city ever had a planning authority. This is Ghosh's "recoupment" in its plainest form: a state body assembles, develops and disposes of land, capturing the value, with no municipal-electoral check.

**The poor next (1970).** The Tamil Nadu Slum Clearance Board (now the TN Urban Habitat Development Board) took slum clearance, eviction and resettlement. The most contentious distributive question in any city — who is evicted, who is rehoused, and how far out (Kannagi Nagar, Perumbakkam, on the far periphery) — was removed from council debate to a state board. "Clearance" liberates valuable central land; the relocation map is a land-value map; and the residents pushed to the edge cannot vote out the board that moved them.

**Planning and land (1971/72).** The Tamil Nadu Town and Country Planning Act, 1971 (TN Act 35 of 1972) created the **CMDA** — master plans, land-use, development control, planning permission, land acquisition across the metropolitan area. This is the depoliticisation move exactly as Ghosh describes it: the rent-rich planning-and-land function lifted out of the elected body and handed to state-appointed technocrats, on the rationale that the work is too "technical" for council politics.

**Water and sewerage (1978).** The single biggest carve-out. The Chennai Metropolitan Water Supply and Sewerage Act, 1978 (TN Act 28 of 1978) gave **Metrowater** exclusive control of water (#5) and sewerage (#6) — the highest-value daily-provisioning functions — to a state board. Contrast Mumbai, Ahmedabad and Kolkata, where the corporation runs water *in-house*. In Chennai a resident cannot vote on a water tariff. The carve-out is affirmatively legislated; it is not a gap.

**Transport, twice.** Buses went to the state-owned MTC (Pallavan Transport Corporation, 1972), never to a municipal undertaking on the BEST or AMTS model. Then in 2007 the metro — the rent-rich transit mode and its station-area land — went to **CMRL**, a Centre-State, JICA-funded SPV, off the municipal books. Melinda Cooper's austerity-extravagance, in one city: the loss-making bus is parked in a perpetually-subsidised state company while capital is lavished on a prestige SPV the citizen cannot vote on. The metro runs past the bus you actually ride.

**Smart-city projects (2016).** Chennai Smart City Ltd is the conditionality move named outright: the Smart Cities Mission *mandated* an SPV as the price of central funds — an unelected company board directing the city's marquee spending, the corporate form imposed top-down to bypass the council.

★ **And then the broom.** The most-hollowed corporation still nominally ran one big thing in-house: **conservancy and solid-waste management** — the labour-heavy, low-rent half Ghosh predicts the elected body is left holding. In the 2020s even that was privatised. **Urbaser-Sumeet** took 7 zones (an 8-year, ~€480M concession signed October 2020, covering ~3.7M people across 92 wards); **Ramky** took ~4 more. By July 2025, **10 of the 15 GCC zones** were under private operators — the most extensive large-city conservancy privatisation in this atlas, with Royapuram and Thiru Vi Ka Nagar next. This lands on Ambedkar's terrain: the predominantly-Dalit, predominantly-women conservancy workforce was transferred from GCC rolls to private firms, **extinguishing a long struggle for permanency and cutting take-home pay from ~₹11,730 to ~₹9,000 a month**. Contractualisation as a caste-precarity machine. And it proceeded under *both* DMK and AIADMK governments — a bipartisan state project, with no meaningful council veto.

Even the corporation's own boundary is state property: the 2011 "Greater Chennai" expansion (42 local bodies merged, 174→426 km²) was an **executive notification under the 1919 Act, not a new statute**. The GCC brand is a state notification, not municipal self-determination.

Eleven carve-outs; five pre-1991, five post-1991, plus the boundary. Seven decided by the state, three by the Centre, the founding charter by the colonial Company — and **zero by the elected city**. The oldest corporation in India holds the broom, and even the broom is now on contract.
