# Ahmedabad: the unelected city, carve-out by carve-out

Ahmedabad keeps an unusually deep in-house estate — water, sewerage, solid waste, roads, fire, the AMTS bus — and so scores well on devolution by Indian standards. That is the misleading half of the story. The other half is that almost every *costly, rent-rich, investor-facing* function has been lifted out of the elected Ahmedabad Municipal Corporation (AMC) into appointed boards, state-and-centre companies, and a private licensee. And in nearly every case **the city did not decide**. The decider was a tier above it: the Bombay/Gujarat legislature, a central scheme, a central Act, or a state company registration. Read against Article 243W — the Constitution's clause that the State Legislature *"may, by law, endow"* municipalities with the eighteen functions — the pattern is exact. Devolution was made optional; Gujarat exercised the option to keep the valuable functions out of the council's reach.

## The pre-1991 wave: state statutes build the parastatals

The developmental-state carve-outs are all **state Acts creating appointed boards**. The Gujarat Housing Board (Act of 1961, successor to the Bombay Housing Board) took planned housing into a statewide board rather than a municipal department. A Gujarat slum-areas framework (from 1973) and its later scheme-driven successors defined slum improvement and urban-poverty alleviation — Twelfth-Schedule items #10 and #11 — above the city, with AMC as implementing agent, not author. Then the two anchors. The **Gujarat Town Planning and Urban Development Act, 1976** (President's Act 27 of 1976) created **AUDA** (1978), handing the city's most valuable function — control of land, planning, and the planned-value uplift of the peri-urban fringe (~1,866 sq km) — to a state-appointed authority. This is Anwesha Ghosh's improvement trust reborn as a development authority: town-planning schemes reconstitute private plots, the authority skims a deduction of serviced land it can monetise, and the planning-gain accrues to a board answerable upward to the state, not to ward voters. The **Gujarat Water Supply and Sewerage Board Act, 1978 (Act 18 of 1979)** created the GWSSB for bulk water and sewerage — though AMC kept the *city* tap, so here the carve-out is real but partial. The state, not the city, drew the line between bulk and last-mile.

The lone counter-move in this wave is the one decision the city actually made: in **1947 Ahmedabad municipalised its buses into AMTS**. That contrast is the whole argument. Where the city chose, it brought transport in-house and kept it. Everything newer routed around it. (Electricity was never the city's to decide: the Ahmedabad Electricity Company was a private licensee from **1915**; Torrent bought out the state's residual stake in 1997 and you cannot vote it out.)

## The post-1991 wave: companies, zones, and central conditionality

After liberalisation the carve-outs stop being boards and become **companies** — SPVs and special zones — increasingly mandated by central schemes. The **Sabarmati Riverfront Development Corporation Ltd (SRFDCL, 2004)**, an AMC–AUDA SPV, is recoupment in its purest modern form: it finances itself by *selling the reclaimed riverbed land it created*, turning public river edge into disposable real estate. The promised rehousing of evicted riverbed communities became, exactly as Ghosh predicts of the improvement trust, the "idle afterthought" — thousands of households displaced, relocation contested. **Ahmedabad Janmarg Ltd (AJL, 2008)** built the award-winning Janmarg BRT as a corporatised SPV because **JNNURM conditionality** required reform-linked, company-form delivery to release central money — a new trunk transit mode built in a vehicle that bypassed AMTS, the council's own struggling bus undertaking, concentrating capital and prestige in the SPV while the everyday bus ran on deficit and a "Loan to AMTS" line. **MEGA Co. → GMRC (2010)** assigned the costliest mode of all, the metro, to a **50:50 Centre–State JICA-funded company in which the elected city holds no seat** — the purest case of routing a municipal-transport function entirely around the council and off its books. The **Ahmedabad Smart City SPV (2015)** exists because the **Smart Cities Mission mandated an SPV** — a company with central and state nominee directors and a deputed CEO — as the precondition for funds: textbook neoliberal conditionality that formally dilutes the elected council's authority over its own territory and concentrates capital in a small area-based enclave (extravagance) while the wider city's directly-funded services are not similarly resourced (Cooper's austerity-extravagance). And **GIFT City (GIFTCL, 2007; IFSCA Act 2019)** takes the logic to its end point: an entire planned "global city" engineered to be governed by a company and a financial regulator, with **no elected council at all**, expressly to make it frictionless for global finance. The unelected city as deliberate design, not accidental drift.

## Beneath the green cells: contractualisation

Even the functions AMC "retains" are hollowed in operation. Door-to-door waste collection runs on private contractors (since 2009); STP operation sits on private O&M contracts; the conservancy front line layers ~5,000 RWA/"community-body" workers atop the permanent cadre — off the contract rolls, doing identical work, overwhelmingly Dalit/Valmiki. CLRA's *Swept Aside* (2025) shows Ahmedabad's Valmikis funnelled into safai work after the mill closures, caste-locked into both permanent and informal rolls. Ambedkar's point exactly — the division of labour as a division of labourers — landing on the corporation's cheapest, dirtiest, most essential function.

## The shape of it

Two waves, one logic, and the city decided almost none of it. Pre-1991 the instruments were state statutes building boards (Housing Board 1961, slum framework 1973, AUDA 1976/78, GWSSB 1979); post-1991 they were companies and zones driven by central conditionality (SRFDCL 2004, AJL 2008, GMRC 2010, Smart City SPV 2015, GIFT 2007/2019). Across both, the decider was always a higher tier. Ahmedabad's strong devolution score is genuine but narrow — a robust in-house estate of *low-rent* services, encircled by an unelected city of land, bulk water, riverfront, transit and finance that the voter cannot touch. The council was left the broom; the rent went elsewhere, by design, decided elsewhere.
