# Bengaluru: the unelected city, by statute

In June 2026, Bengaluru — the country's tech capital — has **no elected municipal government.** The single Bruhat Bengaluru Mahanagara Palike (BBMP) was dissolved in 2025; in its place sit five City Corporations under a state-chaired Greater Bengaluru Authority, with fresh elections still pending. This is no aberration. It is the endpoint of an eighty-year pattern in which **every body that took a function from Bengaluru's elected corporation was created by someone other than the city** — the colonial Mysore state once, the Union once, the Government of Karnataka ten times. The elected city decided none of it. On the axis that matters for a civic-accountability atlas — *who decided* — Bengaluru scores zero.

Two acts, a half-century apart, hold the whole story.

## The CITB → BDA succession: the improvement trust reborn

The first is the cleanest improvement-trust-to-development-authority lineage outside Calcutta. In **1945**, the princely state of Mysore — not the Bangalore municipality — constituted the **City Improvement Trust Board (CITB)** under the City of Bangalore Improvement Act. This was the textbook Ghosh manoeuvre, a generation after Bombay (1898) and Calcutta (1911): lift land and "city improvement" out of elected municipal politics into an appointed executive board that finances itself through land — acquiring cheaply, laying out extensions (Jayanagar and the rest), capturing the uplift. Anwesha Ghosh's phrase fits: "an executive body and not a deliberative organisation." Improvement was the idiom; land-value capture the purpose.

In **1976**, the State of Karnataka did not abolish that regime — it *enlarged* it. The **Bangalore Development Authority Act, 1976 (Karnataka Act 12 of 1976)** dissolved the CITB and re-founded it as the **Bangalore Development Authority (BDA)**, with wider powers of acquisition, layout formation and development scheme. The colonial trust's land-monetising logic was carried, by statute, straight into the modern parastatal. This is Ghosh's "recoupment" running a century on: the BDA became the city's dominant land machine, acquiring peripheral farmland, forming and allotting layouts, and pocketing the uplift — the rent-rich half of the Twelfth Schedule (planning #1, land-use #2) held by an appointed board, while the elected corporation kept the labour-intensive, low-rent half: sweeping, drains, streetlights. The **Karnataka Town and Country Planning Act, 1961 (Act 11 of 1963)** supplies the durable scaffolding, vesting the single most lucrative discretionary lever in urban land — change of land use — in state Planning Authorities, not the ward.

Around this core, the State stacked the rest of the carve-out, almost all pre-1991: **BWSSB** (Bangalore Water Supply and Sewerage Act, 1964 — Mysore Act 36 of 1964) took the costliest, most patronage-laden function, water and sewerage (#5/#6), into an unelected board carrying its own debt and donor conditionality off the corporation's books; **BMRDA** (1985) scaled planning to the region, displacing the elected Metropolitan Planning Committee the 74th Amendment mandates (Art. 243ZE); the **Karnataka Housing Board** (1962) added a second state land developer; and the **Karnataka Slum (Improvement & Clearance) Board** (1973) removed slum #10/#11 from the ward — caste-and-class offloading, since "clearance" falls on Dalit, Muslim and migrant labour and doubles as inner-city land recovery.

The post-1991 additions are the financialised forms of the same logic. **BMTC** (1997, under the Central RTC Act 1950) runs the bus the working majority rides — from the state, not the ward. **BMRCL / Namma Metro** (an SPV incorporated 2006, 50:50 Centre–State) is recoupment in its purest financial form: a ring-fenced company off the elected body's balance sheet, raising JICA and sovereign-backed debt and capturing corridor land value — extravagance for rail-and-real-estate beside austerity for the broom (Cooper). **Bengaluru Smart City Ltd** (c.2017) makes the SPV a *condition* of Union money: MoHUA's Smart Cities Mission mandated a corporatised vehicle with a CEO and appointed board, bypassing the elected corporation for the mission enclave. And the corporation's *last* in-house core function, sanitation, is itself hollowed: SWM flows through contractors and a BBMP company (BSWML), and a Dalit/Valmiki contract workforce — once paid Rs 2,000–5,000 a month through a labour-contractor middleman — sweeps beside the permanent cadre. Bengaluru is the partial counter-story: organising forced regularisation of ~12,650 workers and abolished the middleman (wages to ~Rs 41,000) — but ~10,000 sweepers and ~9,000 truck crew remain on contract. The win measures the default.

## 2025: the State takes not the function but the city

The second decisive act dispenses with subtlety. The **Greater Bengaluru Governance Act, 2024 — Karnataka Act 36 of 2025** (gazetted 24 April 2025, in force 15 May 2025) **dissolved the single BBMP** and re-engineered Bengaluru into **five City Corporations under a Greater Bengaluru Authority chaired by the Chief Minister**, with ~75 members sitting above the corporations and the civic agencies. The stated rationale is benign — a metropolis too large for one body, brought closer to citizens. The political economy is Ghosh's depoliticisation scaled to the level of the city's constitution: the State (a) removes any elected city government for an extended interregnum, running Bengaluru by administrators while elections are deferred — the very "continuance without elections" Art. 243U forbids; (b) recentralises metropolitan coordination in a CM-chaired apex that absorbs the elected MPC's role; and (c) fragments the citywide mandate — and the single Mayor who could contest the state — into five smaller units. Whatever decentralisation's merits, the **decider is the State, the elected city is absent, and the deferral of the vote is the tell.**

**Election status is unverified and flagged as such** — treat any "elected new corporation" claim as pending until a Karnataka State Election Commission notification confirms polls. The fine print (final corporation count, boundaries, ward numbers, BBMP staff/asset transfer) remains in active flux, already amended twice.

The pattern, in one line: across thirteen instruments spanning 1945 to 2025, the State of Karnataka took Bengaluru's land, its water, its planning, its transit and its slums — and then, in 2025, took the city's elected form itself.
