# City histories: when each corporation was born, and why age never protected it

These pages answer one question for every city in the atlas: **when was its municipal corporation actually constituted, under what law, and from which lineage?** The point is not antiquarian. It is the deepest layer of the atlas's argument — that the 74th Amendment (1992) arrived to "empower" elected city governments that, in most cases, were already decades or centuries old, and that the hollowing-out of their powers tracks **not their age** but a later, deliberate carve-out into state boards, development authorities and SPVs.

## The single finding

**Devolution does not track age.** Madras has the oldest corporation in India (1688) and scores 50%. Mumbai's corporation (1888) scores 83%. The difference is not seniority — it is whether, in the parastatal decades after 1960, the state pulled water, transport and land out of the elected body. Where it did (the south, the princely capitals), the corporation was left "holding the broom." Where it didn't (Bombay-Presidency cities), the council still runs the pipes.

## Three lineages

1. **Presidency capitals** — Madras (1688), Calcutta (1726 JPs → 1876), Bombay (1888). The earliest and, on paper, strongest corporations.
2. **Bombay-Presidency cities under the BPMC Act, 1949** — Ahmedabad, Pune, Surat (and Baroda by absorption, Nagpur in spirit). Inherited the strong, capacious template; tend to keep water and sewerage in-house.
3. **Princely-state & provincial capitals** — Hyderabad (Nizam), Bangalore (Mysore), Jaipur (Kachhwaha), Cochin, plus provincial cities (Kanpur, Nagpur) and the planned capital (Bhubaneswar). Separate genealogies; mostly later corporation forms.

Running through all three is the **"improvement regime"** the historian **Anwesha Ghosh** traces (*Municipal Calcutta*, University of Toronto PhD, 2021; "The Improvement Regime," *CSSH*, 2025): the colonial **improvement trust** — a technocratic, land-holding body beside the elected municipality — becomes the modern **development authority** (AUDA, BDA, CMDA, KMDA, MMRDA) that holds the city's land and planning *outside* the council. Nagpur is the live specimen: its improvement trust (NIT, 1936) never even changed its name.

Read the column pairs together: the oldest corporations (Chennai, Kolkata, Mumbai) span the full devolution range (50–83%), and the spread is explained entirely by lineage and the post-1960 carve-out, never by age. Surat and Vadodara are not yet in the data set (no computed score), but their GPMC-Act profile mirrors Ahmedabad's — which is itself the proof that **devolution is set by the state legislature's municipal law, not by the city.**
