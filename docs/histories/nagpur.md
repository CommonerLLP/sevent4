# Nagpur: the city that still has a Trust — the improvement regime, alive

**Incorporated as a Municipal Corporation: 1951** · under the **City of Nagpur Corporation Act, 1948** · devolution score today: **75%** (9 of 12 resident-facing services run by the elected corporation).

## The dates that matter

Nagpur belongs to none of the three usual lineages. It is not a Presidency capital, not a Bombay-Presidency BPMC city, not a princely-state capital. It is a **Central Provinces & Berar** city — a child of *provincial* law, the British administrative middle-country that the standard urban histories tend to skip.

- **1864** — A municipality is constituted at Nagpur. The taxing, sweeping, lamp-lighting body that precedes representative rule.
- **1936** — The **Nagpur Improvement Trust (NIT)** is created. Hold this date. It matters more than any other on the page.
- **1948 / 1951** — Nagpur is incorporated as a **Municipal Corporation** under the **City of Nagpur Corporation Act, 1948**, the corporation coming into being in 1951 (verify) — an elected council to run the city.

A careful note: the corporation is the *younger* body. The Trust is older than the elected corporation it sits beside. That inversion is the whole story.

## The Trust that never became a "development authority"

Anwesha Ghosh's reading of the colonial city is the key that unlocks Nagpur. The British "improvement trust" — an unelected board built to acquire land, plan layouts, and remake the city's geography — did not die at Independence. Across India it survived by **changing its name**: trust became *development authority*. Ahmedabad's became AUDA. The body stayed the same; the word "trust" was quietly retired.

Nagpur is the living specimen — the case where even the rebranding never happened. The **NIT, founded 1936, is still called a Trust.** For nearly a century it has held this city's land and planning, and it has never been folded into the elected corporation, never renamed, never made to answer to a councillor anyone voted for. It runs planning and land *beside* the elected NMC (and shares parks with it — verify). Where other cities preserved the improvement regime under a modern alias, Nagpur preserves it under its own colonial name. It is the museum kept open, the exhibit still operating.

## What was taken

Nagpur scores **75%** — high. The elected NMC runs nine of the twelve services a resident relies on: sewerage, the city bus (Aapli Bus, NMC operator-run — verify), roads, solid waste, street lighting, storm-water, parks, public health, and fire. That is a real council doing real work.

And yet the costliest, most contract-rich functions route around it:

- **Water → Orange City Water (OCW)** — a Veolia PPP with the NMC; the elected body owns the network, a private operator runs it (verify).
- **The metro → Maha Metro / Nagpur Metro**, a special-purpose vehicle, off the municipal books.
- **Land and planning → the NIT**, the 1936 improvement trust that has never let go.

Electricity (MSEDCL) and police are the state's, as everywhere.

So Nagpur teaches the atlas's hardest lesson: **devolution does not track age, and a high score is not a clean one.** A council can run most of the visible city and still not own the ground it stands on. The 74th Amendment was meant to make the land of your city answerable to your vote. In Nagpur, for ninety years, a Trust has answered instead.
