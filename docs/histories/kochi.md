# Kochi: India's strongest municipal law, and pipes the city still doesn't own

**Incorporated as the Corporation of Cochin: 1967** · a merger of **Fort Cochin, Mattancherry, Ernakulam** and others under the Kerala Municipal Corporations framework · devolution score today: **50%** (6 of 12 resident-facing services run by the elected Kochi Corporation).

## The dates that matter

Kochi is not one city's history but several, stitched together late. It is the atlas's clearest case that **devolution does not track age** — a city can be ancient and still not control its own water.

- **1925** — A municipality is constituted (verify). But "Kochi" at this point is not a single municipal body; it is a coastline of separate jurisdictions under two different sovereignties.
- **1967** — The **Corporation of Cochin** is incorporated, *merging* Fort Cochin, Mattancherry, Ernakulam and adjacent areas into one elected body under the Kerala Municipal Corporations framework. The unified city is younger than the Republic — and younger than Ahmedabad's corporation by seventeen years.
- **1976** — The **Greater Cochin Development Authority (GCDA)** is created: a state board that holds the city's land and planning power *outside* the elected corporation.

## A composite city, two colonial lineages

Most cities in this atlas descend from a single legal root. Kochi descends from two, and the seam still shows.

**Fort Cochin** was **Madras Presidency** territory — directly British, on the presidency-capitals branch of the family tree. **Mattancherry** and **Ernakulam** belonged to **princely Cochin/Travancore** — indirectly ruled, on the princely-state branch alongside Hyderabad, Bangalore and Jaipur. These were not neighbourhoods of one city; they were fragments of two different states. They became a single elected corporation only in **1967**.

That late stitching matters, because the pattern Ghosh names — *improvement trust → development authority holds land outside the elected corporation* — arrives here too. The unification of 1967 made an elected Kochi; **GCDA in 1976 immediately routed the land around it.** The corporation was assembled and then, almost at once, hollowed at the most valuable layer.

## What was taken

Kerala has, on paper, **India's strongest municipal-law regime** — it sits at the top of devolution surveys. And yet Kochi's elected corporation controls only half of what a resident relies on.

**Kept in-house (the elected corporation runs these):** roads, solid waste — the **Brahmapuram** plant is a corporation facility, the one that *caught fire in 2023* and smothered the city for days — street lighting, storm-water drainage, parks (with GCDA, verify), and public health.

**Taken out, into state hands:**

- **Water and sewerage → Kerala Water Authority**, a state board. The pipes are not the city's.
- **City bus → KSRTC** (state) and private operators.
- **Metro → KMRL**, a special-purpose vehicle — which also runs the **Kochi Water Metro**.
- **Land and planning → GCDA**, the state board.

Electricity (**KSEB**) and police sit at the state level as context — the surround, not the score.

This is the atlas's sharpest paradox. Strong law on paper, state-held pipes in fact. Kerala wrote the model municipal statute the rest of India is measured against — and Kochi's water and power still answer to the state, not to the council its residents elect. The 74th Amendment said this would be different.
