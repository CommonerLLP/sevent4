# Hyderabad: a Nizam's capital, governed today by everyone but its council

**First municipality: 1869** · **Municipal Corporation of Hyderabad: 1955** · **Greater Hyderabad Municipal Corporation: 2007** · devolution score today: **50%** (6 of 12 resident-facing services run by the elected corporation).

## The dates that matter

- **1869** — Hyderabad gets a municipality. This is not a colonial Presidency town; it is the seat of the Nizam, a princely state running its own affairs. The city built its own municipal and improvement machinery decades before it joined the Indian Union in **1948**. (A "City Improvement Board" is reported around **1912**, but the record is not firm — read it as a sign of the direction, not a fixed date.)
- **1955** — The **Municipal Corporation of Hyderabad** is constituted — seven years *after* accession, under independent India.
- **1975** — **HUDA**, an urban development authority, is created.
- **1989** — Water and sewerage are hived off to **HMWSSB**, a state board.
- **2007** — The **Greater Hyderabad Municipal Corporation** absorbs the surrounding municipalities into one large body.
- **2008** — **HMDA** succeeds HUDA, taking land and planning across the metropolitan region.

## Three ways a city becomes a corporation — and why age tells you nothing

The atlas tracks three lineages into the modern corporation. Some cities descend from **Presidency capitals** — the colonial port-towns of Madras, Bombay, Calcutta. Others are **Bombay-Presidency cities** constituted under the BPMC template (Ahmedabad, Pune, Surat). And a third group are **princely-state capitals** — Hyderabad, Bangalore, Jaipur, Cochin — cities that were governing themselves under a ruler, not a Governor, until accession.

The instinct is to assume the older the council, the deeper its power. Hyderabad disproves it cleanly. Here is a capital with a municipality from **1869** and its own improvement bodies under the Nizam — yet its elected corporation, in its modern form, arrived only in **1955**, under the Republic. Devolution does not track age. It tracks the **parastatal carve-out**, and that carve-out is a *post-1960s* phenomenon — the development authority (HUDA in 1975), the water board (HMWSSB in 1989), the metro company. The pattern Ghosh names recurs: the improvement trust becomes the development authority, and the development authority holds the city's land **outside** the elected corporation — a body the resident never votes for, sitting on the most valuable thing the city owns.

## What was taken

GHMC, the elected body, keeps the visible street-level work in-house: roads (including the SRDP flyovers), solid waste, street lighting, storm-water drains (the nalas), parks, and public health. That is the 50%.

The other half — the capital-heavy, contract-rich, land-holding half — was routed away from the people you elect:

- **Water and sewerage → HMWSSB** (a state board, since 1989).
- **City buses → TSRTC / TGSRTC** (a state transport undertaking).
- **The metro → Hyderabad Metro Rail**, built as an **L&T public-private partnership** — a private concession running a core public service.
- **Land and planning → HMDA** (a state board, 2008).

Electricity (TGSPDCL) and policing sit with the state as context, as they do across the atlas.

So Hyderabad is the lineage stated plainly: a princely capital whose corporation arrived only under independent India, and which then watched its water, its transport, and its land all routed to state boards and a private metro concession. A city this old should not have to ask who runs it. The 74th Amendment said this would be different.
