# Kolkata: the municipality as a corporation — Anwesha Ghosh's thesis, on the ground

**Incorporated as a Municipal Corporation: 1876** · foundational statute the **Calcutta Municipal Act, 1923**; renamed under the Calcutta Municipal Corporation Act, 1980 · devolution score today: **67%** (8 of 12 resident-facing services run by the elected corporation).

## The dates that matter

- **1726** — A Royal Charter brings **Justices of the Peace** to Calcutta, the second city of the British Empire — the earliest municipal authority in the country.
- **1876** — Calcutta gets a **municipal corporation** in recognisable form (a Bengal Act).
- **1911** — The **Calcutta Improvement Trust** is created — the body at the centre of Anwesha Ghosh's history.
- **1923** — The **Calcutta Municipal Act** (associated with C. R. Das) widens the franchise and is the foundational modern statute.
- **1970** — The **KMDA** (Kolkata Metropolitan Development Authority) takes regional planning.
- **2001** — The city is officially renamed Kolkata.

## What the thesis tells us

Kolkata is the city Anwesha Ghosh reconstructs in *Municipal Calcutta: Urban Governance in a Colonial City* (University of Toronto, 2021). Her argument reframes what a municipal corporation *is*. The municipality, she writes, is "constitutively … a corporate body and, like its more recent counterpart, the joint stock company," with a "mode of governing … biased towards economically gainful projects." Rather than a neutral provider of civic goods, the colonial corporation operated through a **"municipal governmentality"** — a set of tactics by which it became "a profitable enterprise even as it presented itself as a liberal benefactor."

The instrument of that enterprise was **property**: the municipality built a colonial "regime of property," remaking ownership through new property laws, bazaar reform, house-rent standardisation and land development — and in doing so produced the **"landlord-entrepreneur,"** a propertied class that used civic representation to defend its holdings. The **Calcutta Improvement Trust (1911)** is the sharp edge of this: a technocratic, financialised land body that, in Ghosh's reading, was "constitutively designed" to secure the built environment — and the land market — rather than the residents. This is the deep root of the **"improvement regime"** that, across India, becomes the modern **development authority** sitting outside the elected council.

So Kolkata is not just one more data point. It is the historical *source* of the pattern the whole atlas measures: the civic body as a property-and-revenue machine, with an improvement trust hived off to run the land.

## What was taken

For all that history, the Kolkata Municipal Corporation still runs an unusually full set in-house — **water, sewerage, roads, solid waste, drainage and pumping, street lighting, parks, public health** — which is the 67%. What has been pulled out is distinctive:

- **The metro → Metro Railway Kolkata**, run by **Indian Railways** — almost unique in India; not even a state SPV, but the central railway.
- **City buses → WBTC** and private operators (state).
- **Land / regional planning → KMDA** (1970) — the lineal descendant of the 1911 Improvement Trust.
- **Electricity → CESC**, privatised (an RP-Sanjiv Goenka company) — like Ahmedabad's Torrent.

The 74th Amendment promised to deepen the elected corporation. In Kolkata it inherited a body that, a century earlier, had already been built — in Ghosh's account — to behave like a company and to govern through property. The Amendment's language of *funds, functions and functionaries* meets a much older municipal logic of *land, rent and revenue.*
