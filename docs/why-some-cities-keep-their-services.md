# Why some cities keep their services and others don't: the institutional history behind the devolution scorecard

The scorecard shows Mumbai (67%) and Ahmedabad (57%) running their core services
in-house while Chennai, Bengaluru, Hyderabad and others score 0% — water, sewerage
and transport all hived off to state boards. This is not random. The corpus explains
it as a documented institutional history, and it vindicates the Bombay-Presidency
intuition.

## The strong colonial Bombay corporation kept services *inside* the elected body

Prashant **Kidambi**, *The Making of an Indian Metropolis: Colonial Governance and
Public Culture in Bombay, 1890–1920* (2007), and his "Planning, the information order,
and the Bombay census of 1901" (2013): colonial Bombay built a **strong, capacious
municipal corporation early** (from the 1860s — rating, civic amenities, its own
information order). Nikhil **Anand**, "Pressure: The PoliTechnics of Water Supply in
Mumbai" (2011): Mumbai's water department was founded in **1860** as an in-house
municipal "Hydraulic Department." Zainab **Bawa**, "Where is the State?" (2011) states
it plainly: *"The Hydraulic Department … is integrated with the Municipal Corporation
of Greater Mumbai."* Water stayed **inside** the elected body.

## The south/east hived services out — by design (the key citation)

Bawa is the direct answer: *"In other large Indian cities, water supply and sewerage
functions have been **separated from the municipal body, organized instead into
independent water boards, headed by senior bureaucrats and ministers at the state
government level** … advocated … to make decision-making expedient by removing it"*
from political/municipal control. Her cited theorist is Karen **Coelho** (2005), the
scholar of India's parastatal water-board "reform." That is Chennai Metrowater, BWSSB,
HMWSSB: a deliberate model of taking services **out** of elected municipalities into
technocratic state boards.

## The deeper genealogy — the improvement trust → development authority

Anwesha **Ghosh**, "The Improvement Regime: Public Trusts, Real Estates, and India's
Urban Futurities" (2025), and **Raju, Kumar, Corbridge & Legg**, "Post-Colonial
Developmentalities: From the Delhi Improvement Trust to the Delhi Development Authority"
(2006): the colonial **improvement trust → development authority** lineage is the
ancestor of AUDA / BDA / CMDA — technocratic land bodies that pull planning out of the
corporation, replacing "proud municipal government" with "another bureaucratic system."
Ravi **Sundaram**, *Pirate Modernity* (2010): planning bodies "pushed for the
privatized decoupling of infrastructures."

## The warning from inside the "high" cities

Mona **Mehta**, "Ahmedabad: the middle-class megacity" (2016): even Ahmedabad's
parastatals are designed to create *"a functional impotence of democratically elected
bodies and [to] encroach upon the constitutionally devolved areas."* So 57% is not a
clean win — the GMRC / AUDA / SPV layer is the **same hollowing, arriving later**.

## The lesson: it's the state's municipal law, not the city

Surat and Vadodara/Baroda run under the **identical GPMC Act, 1949** — the same
Bombay-Presidency legal DNA as Ahmedabad — and so cluster at the same high-devolution
profile (water/sewerage/solid waste/roads in-house; electricity carved to Torrent;
land to SUDA/VUDA). Surat (SMC) is India's most-cited well-run corporation (the
post-1994-plague in-house-delivery turnaround). Conversely every Karnataka / Tamil
Nadu / Telangana city scores near zero because *those states* built parastatals.

So "Bengaluru is a mess" and "Surat is well-run" is really **"Karnataka's vs Gujarat's
municipal-law regime"** — which is exactly why Janaagraha's ASICS 2023 rates the
*state's* urban law, not the individual city. The devolution number is set upstream,
in the State legislature, not in the city.
