# The corporate city: why your municipality was built to take rent, not give service

> *"The Calcutta municipality certainly served as a provider of basic civic services to its
> urban population, but it did not preclude profiteering motives from these public interest
> initiatives. In this, the municipality mirrored the corporate proclivities of the imperial
> trading companies whose primary objective was the accumulation of capital."*
> — **Anwesha Ghosh**, *Municipal Calcutta: Urban Governance in a Colonial City* (PhD, University of Toronto, 2021)

This atlas owes its analytical spine to the historian **Anwesha Ghosh**. Her doctoral thesis on
colonial Calcutta gives us the single idea that makes the whole map legible: **the Indian
municipality is, by constitution, a corporation** — and a corporation's first instinct is not to
serve but to accumulate. We use her framework as scaffolding. Where we extend it from her colonial
archive to today's cities, we say so plainly; the lens is hers, the extension is ours.

## Ghosh's argument, in four moves

**1. The municipality is a corporation — and its ancestor is the rent-collector.** "Corporation" long
predates the modern business firm — it meant any chartered legal person holding a common seal and
property. Ghosh's move (inverting Philip Stern's *Company-State*) is to read the colonial municipality
the way we now read a joint-stock company: an entity whose civic functions never displaced its
**profit motive**. She traces the lineage with startling precision. In 1698 the East India Company
bought the right to collect revenue from the three villages that became Calcutta; the official charged
with it was titled **"Zemindar"** — the rent-collector — and it was *he* who also took on drainage,
wells, street-lighting and water, "activities which would later become identified as 'municipal'."
The Indian municipality, Ghosh shows, "was **not modelled on British town management policies but
rather developed from the range of administrative roles performed by the native zamindar**." Service
was, from birth, the side-duty of a revenue office. As the historian P. T. Nair put it of colonial
Calcutta, the English "looked upon the municipal services **not as a matter of duty or social
commitment, but as avenues of profit**." The Corporation of Madras (1688), the Calcutta and Bombay
corporations — these were trading-company offspring before they were anyone's elected council.

**2. "Municipal governmentality" — benevolence as the *form* of extraction.** Ghosh's key concept.
The colonial municipality did not choose between uplift and profit; it **fused** them, "such that the
task of upliftment itself became a flourishing enterprise." Service was the *idiom*; revenue was the
*purpose*. Crucially, improvement was always **selectively activated** — the art lay in "calibrating
the degree of 'progress' and 'improvement' … based on who were to enjoy these public utilities and
how much the municipality got to gain." Uneven service was not a failure of the system. It *was* the
system.

**3. "Improvement" as technocratic depoliticization.** Following Tania Li, Ghosh shows how
"improvement" reframes political questions — *who gets water? who is evicted?* — as neutral
"technical problems," letting the state "disengage from politics." Its institutional form was the
**Improvement Trust**: Bombay 1898, Calcutta 1911, Delhi 1937 — bodies created expressly to lift
planning and infrastructure **out of the elected municipality** and hand them to **appointed
technocrats**, "to depoliticize the project of developing urban infrastructure from the stranglehold
of municipal politics."

**4. The trust as a rent machine ("recoupment").** Ghosh's sharpest contribution. The Calcutta
Improvement Trust financed itself by **selling surplus land it had acquired and redeveloped**, at
prices set not by present value but by "a profit to a future development that might or might not be
realized" — what Timothy Mitchell calls "rendering the future available in the present." Rehousing
the poor, the trust's founding justification, "became an idle afterthought." The body built to
improve the city became, in Ghosh's reading, **its leading rentier**.

## The scaffolding, applied: India's cities as corporate machines

Here is our extension. The 74th Amendment (1992) promised that your city would be run by an
**elected** corporation with real power over your water, roads, health and money — a
*service-delivering democracy*. But it laid that promise over an institution that, as Ghosh shows,
was never built to serve. The result is the gap this atlas measures, and it has a precise shape:

- **The development authority is the improvement trust, reborn.** AUDA, BDA, CMDA, HMDA, MMRDA, the
  JDA — every one descends, institutionally and in logic, from the colonial improvement trust. Several
  literally are the same body renamed: Bangalore's **CITB (1945) → BDA (1976)**; Nagpur's **NIT
  (1936)** never even changed its name. They hold the city's **land**, and they monetize it. This is
  Ghosh's "recoupment" running a century on.
- **The carve-out is the depoliticization move, repeated.** When a state hives water off to a board
  (Chennai Metrowater 1978, BWSSB 1964), or builds a metro through an off-the-books SPV, the
  justification is always Ghosh's 1911 justification verbatim: rescue "technical" development from
  "the stranglehold of municipal politics." The atlas's **devolution scorecard** is a measure of how
  much of each city has been carved out this way.
- **The elected body is left holding the broom; the rent-rich functions are taken.** Read the
  scorecard with Ghosh's eyes and the pattern is unmistakable. What stays with the elected
  corporation is the **labour-intensive, low-rent** half — sweeping, drains, potholes, streetlights.
  What is pulled into boards and SPVs is the **capital-intensive, rent-rich** half — **land, water
  concessions, transit and its real estate**. Mumbai (83%) kept more inside the council; Chennai and
  Bengaluru (50%) kept the broom and lost the rent. In every case, *where service and rent diverge,
  rent wins* — exactly as Ghosh's colonial municipality chose its improvements by "how much the
  municipality got to gain."
- **Service was always the by-product, never the point.** This is the hard claim the data supports.
  The metro that runs past the bus you actually ride; the development authority that auctions land
  while the ward has no library; the water board that serves the colony but not the settlement — none
  of these are malfunctions. They are a corporate machine doing what Ghosh shows it was built to do:
  turn the city into rent, and offer service only where, and only because, it pays.

## Why the 74th Amendment cuts against the grain

This is the deepest reason the Amendment's promise has gone unkept. It asked a **corporation** to
behave like a **democracy of service** — to put funds, functions and functionaries under people you
elect, and to treat water and health as rights rather than yields. That is not a small reform. It is
an attempt to reverse the founding logic Ghosh traces: to make the rent machine serve. The atlas
documents how completely the machine has resisted — not through scandal, but through *structure*: the
quiet, technocratic, century-old habit of moving the valuable functions just out of the elected
body's reach, and calling it improvement.

And note the word itself. **"Devolution"** is the 74th Amendment's promise — and it is also exactly
what the colonial state claimed to be doing a century earlier. Ghosh, reading the founding of the
Calcutta Improvement Trust in 1911, quotes the historian Partho Datta: *"The imperial authorities had
been keen to foist the costs of government to the local populace in the name of devolution."* Her own
verdict is sharper still: *"The work of devolution itself was a travesty since the local populace
continually faced the ordeal of higher and higher taxes without any hope of real representation."*
That sentence was written about 1911. It is also, precisely, what this atlas measures in 2026. When a
state hands a city its potholes and its property tax but keeps its water, its land and its metro, it
is performing the older meaning of "devolution" — downloading the *costs* of government while
withholding the *power*. The Improvement Trust was built to be **"an executive body and not a
deliberative organisation,"** with the elected corporation reduced to four seats of eleven on its
board. Read that description again and you have not a colonial relic but a job-description for AUDA,
BWSSB, CMDA, the GMRC — every unelected body in the scorecard.

To read your city's wards, its carved-out services and its unelected officers through this lens is to
stop asking "why is my city so badly run?" and start asking Ghosh's question instead: **badly run for
whom, and profitably run for whom?**
