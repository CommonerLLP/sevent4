# Kolkata (KMC) and Chennai (GCC): devolution and governance status

A 74th-Amendment / devolution and governance dossier for two eastern and southern
metros. The 74th Amendment's 12th Schedule lists 18 functions that elected
municipal bodies are meant to perform. The atlas's recurring finding is that in
most Indian cities these functions are **scattered across state boards, parastatals,
and SPVs that the elected corporation does not control** — so a "city government"
exists in name while the city's actual services answer upward to the state (or, in
one striking case, to the central government). Kolkata and Chennai are two ends of a
spectrum: Kolkata keeps relatively more in-house but is fragmented on transport and
power; Chennai's elected corporation runs almost nothing, yet has the strongest
record of *political* (electoral) devolution. Status current to mid-2026; sourced.

---

## Kolkata Municipal Corporation (KMC), West Bengal

**Council status.** KMC has an elected council (144 wards). Trinamool Congress (TMC)
won the December 2021 corporation election in a landslide. **Firhad Hakim** —
mayor since November 2018 and the city's first Muslim mayor since Independence —
**resigned on 5 June 2026** amid post-poll turmoil in the TMC following a poor state
showing, saying he did not want "to hold the chair for the sake of it"; Mamata
Banerjee accepted the resignation. (Reporting in the same period noted BJP gains
across KMC wards; the council's next configuration is in flux.)

**Functional fragmentation — what KMC does and doesn't control.**
- **Water supply + sewerage/drainage: in-house.** Unlike most Indian metros, KMC
  runs its own water-supply and sewerage-and-drainage departments rather than a
  separate state water board. This makes Kolkata **relatively more devolved** on the
  core 12th-Schedule water/sanitation functions.
- **Electricity: private (CESC).** Power distribution in the KMC area is run by
  **CESC Ltd** (RP-Sanjiv Goenka Group), a private licensee — outside both the
  corporation and the state utility.
- **City bus transport: state (WBTC).** The **West Bengal Transport Corporation**,
  a state undertaking, runs city buses — not KMC.
- **Metro rail: the central government (Indian Railways).** The **Kolkata Metro is
  operated by Metro Railway, Kolkata — the 17th zone of Indian Railways**, wholly
  owned and funded by the Union Ministry of Railways (formalised as a zone in 2010;
  the only metro in India run directly by Indian Railways). The city's rapid-transit
  backbone thus answers neither to the corporation nor even to the state, but to New
  Delhi. New lines are built by **Kolkata Metro Rail Corporation (KMRC)**, a Union
  Government enterprise (2008).
- **Metropolitan planning: state (KMDA).** Master-planning and major development for
  the metro region sit with the **Kolkata Metropolitan Development Authority**, a
  state parastatal — not the elected corporation.

**Financial autonomy.** KMC's larger own-source base (notably property tax and water
charges, since water is in-house) gives it more fiscal substance than corporations
that have outsourced water to a board; but like all Indian ULBs it remains dependent
on state transfers and on the State Finance Commission cycle, and capital works flow
heavily through KMDA and state schemes rather than the corporation's own budget.

**Empowered-mayor question.** West Bengal runs a **mayor-in-council** system, which
makes the Kolkata mayor more executive-substantive than the one-year ceremonial
mayors of many states — Hakim held the office continuously for years and wielded
real administrative weight. But the mayor is still **indirectly elected** by
councillors, not directly by citizens, and key services (transport, power, metro,
planning) lie outside the corporation he leads.

**State devolution record.** West Bengal has constituted the 74th-Amendment
machinery on paper — ward committees, a State Election Commission, State Finance
Commissions, and District/Metropolitan Planning Committees (the state is among those
that have set up DPCs). Academic assessments (e.g. Manabi Majumdar's *Delegation to
Devolution*) and Janaagraha's city-systems surveys nonetheless place real
decision-power with the state and its parastatals (KMDA), with ward committees and
planning committees weak in practice.

## Greater Chennai Corporation (GCC), Tamil Nadu

**Council status.** After **six years with no elected council (administrator rule,
2016–2022)**, GCC's elected council was restored by the **2022 Tamil Nadu local
elections**, won by the DMK. **R. Priya (Priya Rajan)** was elected mayor on 4 March
2022 — at 28, the **youngest and the first Dalit woman mayor of Chennai** (third
woman overall). India's oldest municipal corporation (1688) thus pairs a landmark
in *political* representation with one of the country's emptiest functional mandates.

**Functional fragmentation — GCC runs almost nothing.**
- **Water + sewerage: state board (CMWSSB / "Metrowater").** The **Chennai
  Metropolitan Water Supply and Sewerage Board** (1978 Act) — a state board — holds
  water and sewerage, not the corporation.
- **City bus transport: state (MTC).** The **Metropolitan Transport Corporation** is
  a state undertaking.
- **Metro rail: state SPV (CMRL).** **Chennai Metro Rail Limited**, a central–state
  joint venture/SPV; suburban rail is Southern Railway. Neither is GCC's.
- **Metropolitan planning: state (CMDA).** The **Chennai Metropolitan Development
  Authority** (under the TN Town & Country Planning Act, 1971) prepares the master
  plan — not the corporation.
- **Electricity** sits with the state utility (TANGEDCO), and **police** with the
  state. GCC is effectively left with roads, solid-waste, streetlights, drains,
  parks, and property tax — a **near-zero functional-devolution scorecard** against
  the 12th Schedule.

**Financial autonomy.** With water revenue gone to CMWSSB and transport/metro/planning
to state agencies, GCC's own-revenue base is thin relative to the services people
associate with "the city"; it is heavily reliant on state grants and schemes.

**Empowered-mayor question — Tamil Nadu's distinctive *political* devolution.** TN is
the counter-case on the *electoral* side: it has experimented with **directly
elected mayors** (direct election scrapped in 2006 for indirect election among
councillors, restored in 2011 on the Election Commission's recommendation, and the
2022 cycle reverted to indirect election by councillors). It also produced the rare
empowered Indian mayor — **M.K. Stalin, Mayor of Chennai 1996–2002** — though that
clout flowed from a dynastic link to the CM, not from the office. The **2016–2022
administrator gap** shows the other face: even strong urban *institutions* did not
prevent six years with no elected city government at all.

**State devolution record.** Tamil Nadu has **strong, capable urban institutions**
(CMWSSB, CMDA, MTC, CMRL are well-established parastatals) and a notable record on
*representation* (reservation, the first Dalit woman mayor). But this is precisely
**institutional strength without functional devolution to the elected body**: the
parastatals are competent, and that competence is one reason the state has little
incentive to hand the functions to the corporation.

---

## The sharpest contrast

Both cities scatter their services, but they fail the 74th Amendment in *opposite*
directions. **Kolkata is relatively more functionally devolved** — KMC actually runs
its own water and sewerage and has a more substantive mayor-in-council — yet its
transport and power are external and its **metro is run by the central government's
Indian Railways**, a fragmentation so extreme the city's rail backbone answers to New
Delhi, not even to the state. **Chennai is the inverse**: its elected corporation
runs almost nothing (water, bus, metro, planning all state-held → roughly **0%
functional devolution**), and yet Tamil Nadu has the stronger record of *political*
devolution — directly-elected (then scrapped) mayors, India's first Dalit woman
mayor. Put plainly: **Kolkata gives its elected body more to do but scatters the rest
upward (even to the Centre); Chennai gives its elected body more democratic
legitimacy but almost nothing to govern.** Strong institutions are not the same as
devolved power — Chennai proves a state can have excellent urban parastatals and still
hollow out the elected corporation.
