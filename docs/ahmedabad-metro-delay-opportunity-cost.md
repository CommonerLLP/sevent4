# Costing a transport-project delay: the Ahmedabad Metro Phase 1 case

A working method note for the SevenT4 atlas. It sets out how to cost the *delay*
of a public transport project — not just the headline cost overrun — using
current public-finance and transport-appraisal practice, and applies it,
illustratively, to the Ahmedabad Metro Phase 1 delay. Every figure is flagged
as sourced, derived, or assumed. This is a transparent appraisal of public
documents, not an audit finding.

## Why "extra capex ÷ bus budget" is not enough

The intuitive move — "the ₹2,000 cr overrun could have bought N buses" — is a
real and useful figure, but it captures only one of several costs a delay
imposes. Best practice (HM Treasury *Green Book* 2022; World Bank/ADB transport
economic appraisal; Flyvbjerg on megaproject performance) decomposes the cost of
a delayed public project into five components:

1. **Direct cost escalation** (the capex overrun), split into real scope change
   vs. pure price inflation.
2. **Opportunity cost of capital locked up** during the delay — capital sunk in
   a non-operating asset earns the city nothing while the social discount rate
   keeps running.
3. **Deferred benefits** — every year of delay forgoes that year's user benefits
   (time savings, vehicle-operating-cost savings, decongestion, emissions,
   accident reduction, fare revenue).
4. **Marginal cost of public funds (MCF)** — every rupee of public money carries
   a deadweight cost above ₹1 because it is raised through taxation/borrowing.
5. **Distributional weighting** — a rupee of benefit to a poorer traveller
   carries a higher social welfare weight than to a richer one.
6. **Social and environmental cost of the prolonged construction window** —
   dust, congestion, emissions, and disruption to the corridor economy, each
   scaling with delay-years and falling regressively.
7. **Risk allocation in the financing structure** — who actually bears
   completion, overrun, demand, and FX risk, versus who collects regardless of
   project performance.

## Parameters (anchored to live data, 2026-06)

| Parameter | Value used | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Social discount rate (SDR), real | **5%** base; 3% / 8% / 12% sensitivity | 10-yr G-Sec 6.85% and 30-yr 7.0% (CCIL/RBI via finstack) less RBI-target inflation ~4%; World Bank real rate 2.52%; legacy Indian appraisal hurdle 12% as the high bound; ADB economic opportunity cost of capital ~9–12%. |
| Inflation (for nominal→real) | **~4–5%/yr** | CPI 4.95% (2024, World Bank); RBI target 4%, band 2–6%. |
| Marginal cost of public funds | **1.25** (1.15–1.30) | Standard India MCF estimates; Green Book convention that public funds are not free. |
| Elasticity of marginal utility (for distributional weights) | **1.3** | Green Book 2022 Annex 3. |

## The Ahmedabad Metro Phase 1 facts (sourced)

- **Approved cost:** ₹10,773 cr (39.3 km, 32 stations).
- **Actual / escalated cost:** ~₹12,700 cr — an overrun of **~₹2,000 cr (~18%)**.
- **Delay:** original target 2018; full Phase 1 commissioned end-2022 →
  **~5 years late.** Cited causes: ₹600 cr to Indian Railways for land; relief &
  rehabilitation rising ₹125 cr → ₹325 cr; COVID-19.
- **A ₹211 cr scam was *alleged in the press* to involve the then-MD** (a retired
  IAS officer), who was replaced; the project was split into two phases. Treated
  here as a reported allegation, not an adjudicated finding — it is **not** used
  as a quantified cost.
- **Realised benefits were low for years:** monthly ridership never exceeded
  ~38,000 through to mid-2022 (≈1,250/day); some lines ran near-empty. Ridership
  only reached ~1.5 lakh/day by Dec 2025.

## Applying the method (illustrative)

**(A) Direct overrun.** ₹2,000 cr nominal. Flyvbjerg's reference class for rail
puts the *mean* overrun near 45%, so ~18% is, if anything, below type — but it is
measured in mixed-year rupees and mixes real scope (the ₹600 cr Railways land,
the +₹200 cr R&R) with price inflation over the slip. A clean real-terms split
needs the DPR cost base by year (data gap).

**(B) Opportunity cost of capital locked during the delay.** Capital sunk in a
half-built, non-operating metro yields the city nothing while social returns
accrue elsewhere. The textbook cost-of-delay ≈ committed capital × SDR × delay.
On the ~₹10,773 cr base over 5 years:

| SDR | Forgone social return over the 5-yr delay |
|---|---|
| 3% | ≈ ₹1,600 cr |
| **5% (base)** | **≈ ₹2,700 cr** |
| 8% | ≈ ₹4,300 cr |
| 12% (legacy hurdle) | ≈ ₹6,500 cr |

**(C) Deferred user benefits.** Normally the dominant term. Here it is *small*
in the early years — because the asset itself underperformed (ridership a
fraction of forecast). That is not a reprieve for the project; it is an
indictment of the *selection*: a delay is cheap in forgone benefits only when the
benefits were thin to begin with. Quantifying this needs the DPR's annual
economic-benefit stream (data gap), but directionally it is low.

**(D) Marginal cost of public funds.** The ₹2,000 cr overrun is public money;
grossed up at MCF 1.25 its social cost is **≈ ₹2,500 cr**. (This is where the
common "₹2,500 cr" figure finds a rigorous home — not as embezzlement, but as
the overrun valued at the true social cost of public funds.)

**(E) Distributional weight.** Metro riders skew higher-income and longer-trip;
bus/BRTS riders are poorer. Under Green Book income weighting, the *same* rupees
deliver more weighted welfare through AMTS/Janmarg than through the metro,
widening the opportunity-cost gap in favour of buses.

## Headline

Combining the overrun (MCF-adjusted, **~₹2,500 cr**) with the capital-lockup
opportunity cost of the 5-year delay (**~₹2,700 cr** at the 5% base, up to
~₹6,500 cr at the legacy hurdle), the social cost of the Phase 1 delay is of the
order of **₹5,000–8,000 cr** — before counting deferred benefits.

Set against the city's own mobility budget (AMTS ≈ ₹525 cr/yr; Janmarg/BRTS
≈ ₹143 cr/yr), that is roughly **a decade or more of the entire city bus
programme** — and the same capital in buses/BRTS would have scored higher on both
efficiency (benefit–cost ratio) and equity (distributional weight).

## Social and environmental costs of the delay

A delay does not just defer benefits; it **extends the construction-disbenefit
window**. Every extra year of an unfinished corridor is an extra year of:

- **Construction dust (PM10/PM2.5)** along the alignment — a respiratory-health
  cost, monetised in appraisal via morbidity/mortality (VSL) load on the exposed
  roadside population.
- **Traffic snarls / diversions** — excess vehicle-hours travelled, valued at
  value-of-time plus extra vehicle operating cost and fuel.
- **Emissions** — the prolonged congestion (and the construction plant itself)
  burns fuel that would not otherwise be burnt; monetised at a shadow price of
  carbon (Indian appraisals increasingly apply one; range ~₹3,000–7,000/tCO₂).
- **Severance and disruption to the corridor's informal economy** — street
  vendors, small shops, and pedestrians lose footfall and access for the whole
  build period.

Each channel scales roughly linearly with delay-years, so a 5-year slip is a
~5× construction-externality bill relative to an on-time build. Two features make
this worse than a neutral cost:

1. **The incidence is regressive.** Dust, congestion, and vendor disruption fall
   on roadside workers, pedestrians, and bus-dependent commuters — poorer than
   the metro's eventual (richer, longer-trip) ridership. Under distributional
   weighting, these costs carry a *higher* welfare weight than the deferred
   benefits do. The delay transfers cost from a richer future ridership to a
   poorer present population.
2. **It compounds the selection problem.** You eat extra years of dust and
   congestion to deliver an asset that then runs near-empty — paying a real
   social-environmental price for a thin benefit stream.

## Financing structure: who bears the risk, and who is "not worried"

This is the structural core of the argument. Ahmedabad Metro is financed largely
by a **JICA (Japan) ODA loan** to the Government of India, on-lent to the state
SPV. The terms look generous — low coupon (~1–1.5%), long tenor (~30–40 years),
~10-year grace. But generous *pricing of the loan* is not the same as the lender
*sharing the project's risk*. Under this structure:

- **Repayment is a sovereign obligation, decoupled from project performance.**
  Whether the metro is late, over budget, or runs near-empty, the lender is
  repaid in full on schedule. The market discipline that is supposed to punish a
  bad project — default risk, repricing — is muted by the sovereign guarantee.
  So a delay genuinely **costs the lender nothing**; it costs only the public.
- **The public bears every real risk:** completion/delay risk, cost-overrun
  risk, demand/ridership risk, operations-and-maintenance risk, and **foreign-
  exchange risk** (the loan is yen-denominated; whichever way the rupee moves
  over decades, the borrower — not Japan — carries it).
- **Part of the "aid" is recaptured by the lender economy.** JICA's STEP loans
  carry a Japanese-content procurement condition (rolling stock, consultants,
  technology), so a share of the disbursement returns to Japanese firms as export
  demand. The concessional coupon buys influence over *how the money is spent*.
- **Net effect:** the public services the debt for a generation regardless of
  whether the asset works, while the upside of the concession (procurement,
  geopolitical relationship, guaranteed return) accrues to the lender. The only
  remaining check on delay and overrun is **domestic political accountability** —
  which is structurally weak precisely because the asset sits in an SPV outside
  the elected municipal body's control.

A delay, then, is not a shared misfortune. It is a one-sided transfer: the lender
keeps collecting, the contractors are paid, and the public absorbs the overrun,
the deferred and thin benefits, the prolonged dust and congestion, and decades of
interest — for a decision it never controlled.

## The governance point this serves

The decisive fact is *who controlled the money*. The ₹10,773 cr metro and its
~₹2,000 cr overrun sat in a Gujarat-government-owned special-purpose vehicle
(MEGA → GMRC), funded by central and JICA loans, run by appointed officers. The
elected municipal corporation controlled none of it — only the ₹525 cr city bus
line. The most capital-intensive mobility decision in the city was the one least
accountable to the city's voters. That is the 74th-Amendment devolution gap made
quantitative.
