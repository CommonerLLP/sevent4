# Bengaluru: the mess

Among India's major cities, Bengaluru is the sharpest single case of 74th-Amendment
failure. It is the country's tech capital and its best-documented "civic-data" city,
yet on the actual democratic substance of Part IXA — an elected council with devolved
powers — it scores at or near the bottom. This document records the facts, with
sources and dates, neutrally.

## 1. No elected council since 2020 — an ~11-year democratic gap

- The last Bruhat Bengaluru Mahanagara Palike (BBMP) council was elected in **August
  2015** (the [2015 BBMP election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_Bruhat_Bengaluru_Mahanagara_Palike_election)).
  Corporators served a five-year term.
- That term **ended on 10 September 2020**. Since then BBMP has had **no elected
  council**. The State stepped in to appoint a senior IAS officer as administrator
  (Gaurav Gupta was the first), and the corporation has been run by state-appointed
  administrators and chief commissioners ever since
  ([Citizen Matters](https://citizenmatters.in/why-bbmp-council-election-delay-administrator-appointed-scams-accountability-access/)).
- The first poll for the successor bodies is scheduled only **by August 2026**, which
  the record describes as "ending the unprecedented delay of **11 years** since the
  2015 BBMP election"
  ([Wikipedia: 2026 GBA elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Greater_Bengaluru_Authority_elections)).

For roughly a third of the period since 2015, and for an entire continuous stretch
since September 2020, a city of ~9 million voters has been governed by officers
appointed by the State, answerable upward to the State government, not downward to an
elected council the residents chose.

## 2. The Greater Bengaluru Governance Act, 2024 — restructuring as further centralisation

The Congress government, elected in 2023, replaced BBMP rather than reviving it.

- **The Greater Bengaluru Governance Bill, 2024** was introduced on **23 July 2024**
  and passed (after a Joint Select Committee) on **10 March 2025**; the Governor first
  returned it for reconsideration ([PRS India](https://prsindia.org/bills/states/the-greater-bengaluru-governance-bill-2024)).
  It came into force and **BBMP was abolished**, with the Greater Bengaluru Authority
  (GBA) taking over from **2 September 2025**
  ([Citizen Matters](https://citizenmatters.in/bengaluru-local-governance-greater-bengaluru-authority-city-corporation/);
  [The News Minute](https://www.thenewsminute.com/karnataka/gba-to-replace-bbmp-from-sept-2-maheshwar-rao-will-be-new-commissioner)).
- BBMP is replaced by **five city corporations** — Bengaluru Central, North, South,
  East, and West — sitting under an apex **Greater Bengaluru Authority**. Final ward
  delimitation, notified **19 November 2025**, set **369 wards** across the five
  corporations (up 171 from the 198 wards of 2015): Central 63, North 72, South 72,
  East 50, West 112
  ([Deccan Herald](https://www.deccanherald.com/india/karnataka/bengaluru/greater-bengaluru-areas-five-corporations-to-have-369-wards-3803924);
  [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Greater_Bengaluru_Authority_elections)).
- The GBA is **chaired by the Chief Minister**; its Executive Committee is chaired by
  the Bengaluru Development Minister; the **Chief Commissioner** (member secretary,
  appointed by the State for three years) is the principal executive officer for
  contracts, property and budget. The first Chief Commissioner is **M. Maheshwar Rao,
  IAS** (Karnataka 1995 batch), appointed in **August 2025**, holding the rank of an
  Additional Chief Secretary
  ([PRS India](https://prsindia.org/bills/states/the-greater-bengaluru-governance-bill-2024);
  [The Print](https://theprint.in/india/governance/bengalurus-new-chief-commissioner-m-rao-is-harvard-grad-who-has-worked-with-shivakumar-in-past/2609696/)).
- First **GBA / city-corporation elections** are expected **by August 2026**
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Greater_Bengaluru_Authority_elections)).

**The centralisation critique.** The reform is widely read not as devolution but as
its opposite. PRS notes the structure has the Chief Minister chairing both the GBA and
the Metropolitan Planning Committee, giving "the state government a direct say in
municipal governance," with city corporations needing State approval for property
sales, contracts and regulations, and appointed commissioners exercising executive
powers traditionally held by elected mayors — concerns critics frame as conflicting
with the 74th Amendment's decentralisation principle
([PRS India](https://prsindia.org/bills/states/the-greater-bengaluru-governance-bill-2024)).
The argument is bipartisan in form: opposition MP Tejasvi Surya called it "old wine in
a new bottle" delivering "five powerless corporations, five ceremonial Mayors, no real
devolution of finances or authority," with "power [continuing] to flow from the
top — from the Chief Minister and Deputy Chief Minister, not from the people upward"
([Social News XYZ](https://www.socialnews.xyz/2025/10/11/greater-bengaluru-governance-act-meant-for-reforms-ends-up-centralising-power-tejasvi-surya/)).
The substantive point, independent of party: an apex authority chaired by the CM
concentrates power **upward at the State**, arguably weakening municipal
self-government further rather than restoring it.

## 3. Total parastatal fragmentation — the elected corporation controls almost nothing

Even when an elected council exists, the core city services are run by State-controlled
parastatals that are not under the municipal corporation and are answerable only to the
State government:

| Service | Agency | Control |
|---|---|---|
| Water & sewerage | **BWSSB** (Bangalore Water Supply & Sewerage Board) | State |
| Electricity | **BESCOM** (Bangalore Electricity Supply Company) | State |
| Bus transport | **BMTC** (Bangalore Metropolitan Transport Corporation) | State |
| Metro rail | **BMRCL** (Bangalore Metro Rail Corporation Ltd) | State / Centre JV |
| Land & development | **BDA** (Bangalore Development Authority) | State |

None of these sit under the elected corporation. As Citizen Matters puts it, bodies
like BESCOM and BWSSB have no locally elected representatives on their boards and are
"not held accountable to the BBMP but are only answerable to the state government"
([Citizen Matters: Bangalore's civic agencies](https://citizenmatters.in/1968-all-about-bangalore-civic-agencies/)).
Janaagraha's Annual Survey of India's City-Systems describes Bengaluru's "many
governments" syndrome — 30-plus agencies controlling water, transport, electricity,
environment and policing — with most of the 18 constitutionally devolved functions
carried out by parastatals rather than the local body
([Janaagraha ASICS](https://www.janaagraha.org/asics/);
[Decentralization.net](https://decentralization.net/2023/11/annual-survey-of-indias-city-systems-asics-2023-report/)).
On the substance of devolution, the elected city government controls close to nothing —
effectively a 0% devolution score on the services residents actually use.

## 4. The delimitation / reservation litigation and repeated delays

The 11-year gap was produced by a chain of legal and administrative freezes, not a
single decision:

- Elections due in **2020** were delayed by COVID-19, then by an unfinished **ward
  delimitation** and an unresolved **SC/ST/OBC and women reservation** exercise
  ([The Week](https://www.theweek.in/news/india/2025/07/29/will-bengaluru-finally-hold-its-civic-polls-by-december-bbmp-bifurcation-delimitation-and-other-hurdles-explained.html)).
- In **March 2022** the Karnataka High Court directed the State Election Commission to
  hold polls once reservation was finalised; the State notified the final ward
  reservation list only about a year later.
- The **Supreme Court** then stayed the BBMP polls pending the legality of the
  reservation process, and gave the State eight weeks to notify ward delimitation and
  the OBC quota percentage
  ([LiveLaw](https://www.livelaw.in/top-stories/supreme-court-bbmp-karantaka-delimitation-of-wards-reservation-elections-199653)).
- Multiple PILs (reported as around seven) challenged the delimitation of 243 wards,
  alleging violations of the rotation principle and flaws in OBC allocation
  ([The Week](https://www.theweek.in/news/india/2025/07/29/will-bengaluru-finally-hold-its-civic-polls-by-december-bbmp-bifurcation-delimitation-and-other-hurdles-explained.html)).
- Finally, the **replacement of BBMP with the GBA** itself reset the clock: new
  corporations, new boundaries, fresh delimitation (369 wards, notified November 2025),
  and elections pushed to **by August 2026**
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Greater_Bengaluru_Authority_elections)).

Whatever the legal merits of each step, the cumulative *effect* was to keep elected
local government suspended for over a decade while State-appointed officers governed.

## 5. The irony: best civic-data city, worst democratic devolution

Bengaluru is routinely described as India's technology capital and is arguably its
best city for open civic data — ward maps, delimitation files and agency data are
published and reusable (e.g. the
[OpenCity 369-ward delimitation dataset](https://data.opencity.in/dataset/gba-wards-delimitation-2025)).
And yet, on the thing the 74th Amendment actually guarantees — an elected municipal
body with devolved functions and finances — it performs worst among India's major
cities: an 11-year council vacuum, near-total parastatal control of services, and a
2024–25 restructuring that critics across the spectrum read as moving power *up* to the
State rather than *down* to the ward. The city that can map every problem to a ward is
also the city with no elected ward representative to send the problem to. That gap —
abundant civic data, absent civic democracy — is the mess.
