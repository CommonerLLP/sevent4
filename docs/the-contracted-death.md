# The contracted death: how manual scavenging survives its own ban

Manual scavenging is the moral core of this atlas — the point where *who governs* becomes *who dies*. It is
also the sharpest expression of the atlas's contractualisation layer: the corporation's one undeniable
"in-house" function, sanitation, is in practice a **contracted death sentence for Dalit men.** This page
lays out the whole story — the law, the ongoing practice, the mechanism that keeps it alive, the per-city
picture, and the test that actually matters — as the basis for the site's manual-scavenging component.

> Data: `data/institutions/manual_scavenging_map.json` (per-city) and `contractualization_map.json` (the
> sanitation workforce). Frame: Ambedkar (the division of labour as a division of *labourers*), with the
> contracting analysis from `the-double-extraction.md`.

## 1 · The law — banned for three decades, still killing

| Year | Instrument | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| 1950 | **Article 17**, Constitution | Abolished untouchability — but no penal prohibition of the practice. |
| **1993** | **Employment of Manual Scavengers and Construction of Dry Latrines (Prohibition) Act** (Act 46 of 1993) | **First law to criminalise *employing* a manual scavenger** and building/maintaining a dry latrine (jail up to 1 yr + fine). Weak: enacted under Art. 252 (only in states that adopted it), narrowly tied to "dry latrines," **near-zero convictions.** |
| **2013** | **Prohibition of Employment as Manual Scavengers and their Rehabilitation Act** | Replaced 1993; **nationwide**; **broadened** to ban hazardous manual cleaning of **sewers & septic tanks** without gear; added **rehabilitation** duties. |
| 2014 | **Safai Karamchari Andolan v. Union of India** | Supreme Court: **₹10 lakh** compensation per sewer death (back to 1993); identification + rehabilitation. |
| 2023 | **Dr Balram Singh v. Union of India** | SC raised compensation to **₹30 lakh** (death); ₹10–20 lakh (disability); **mandated mechanisation**; 14 eradication directions. |
| **2025** | SC order (29 Jan) | Ordered an end to manual sewer cleaning in **six metros — Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai, Kolkata, Bengaluru, Hyderabad** (five of our cities). |
| 2023– | **NAMASTE** scheme | Mechanisation + PPE + worker profiling (replaced SRMS). |

**Criminalised in 1993, re-criminalised and broadened in 2013 — and still killing in 2025.** That is the
indictment in one line.

## 2 · The ongoing practice — three live forms

1. **Dry-latrine cleaning** — scooping human excreta from insanitary latrines by hand. Survives in rural and
   small-town pockets and, historically, on the **Indian Railways** (open-discharge train toilets cleaned off
   the tracks by hand; the bio-toilet rollout has shrunk but not ended it). Largely **Dalit women.**
2. **Sewer & manhole cleaning** — men descending into municipal sewers without gear, dying of toxic-gas
   (H₂S / methane) asphyxiation. **Most reported deaths.**
3. **Septic-tank & STP cleaning** — in private buildings, apartments, hotels, factories — increasingly the
   main death site, cleaned by informally-hired men off the books.

## 3 · Why it survives the ban — the contract mechanism

The municipality or water board almost never does sewer cleaning with its **own permanent staff** anymore.
The chain runs:

> **Corporation / water board → outsources to a contractor → contractor hires a daily-wage Dalit worker →
> no record, no training, no PPE → a blockage needs clearing → the man is sent *into* the pit (cheaper than
> a machine) → he dies of gas.**

Because he was a **sub-contracted, off-the-books, daily-wage worker**, the liability trail evaporates: the
corporation says *"not our employee,"* the contractor says *"he went in on his own,"* there is no FIR under
the 2013 Act, and so — across the whole country — **almost no convictions.** The corporation "complies" with
the ban *on paper* (it doesn't *employ* scavengers) precisely because the **contractor it hired does the
illegal thing.** The contract form is the **laundering mechanism**: it lets the forbidden act keep happening
while making *no one legally responsible.*

That is why the deaths fall **not on the permanent municipal cadre** (who have unions, status, and the
standing to refuse) **but on the contract / informal layer.** Manual scavenging did not survive the ban
*despite* contractualisation — it survives *through* it.

## 4 · The death migrates as cities mechanise

As the **public manholes** get Bandicoot robots — the photogenic, press-released part — the dying simply
moves to **private septic tanks, apartment STPs, and factory pits**, cleaned by the same caste of contract
men, off the official radar. A city can own robots *and* keep killing: the machines cover the **visible
public** network, the contract worker covers the **invisible private** one.

## 5 · Who, how many, and the official denial

- **Who:** ~**97% of the dead are Scheduled Caste**, predominantly **Valmiki / Balmiki** and regional jatis
  (Arunthathiyar in Tamil Nadu, Madiga/Mala in Telangana, Hela/Mehtar in the north). NAMASTE profiling of
  ~85,000 sanitation workers: ~69% SC, ~92% SC/ST/OBC. Dry-latrine cleaners are largely **Dalit women**;
  sewer/septic deaths are **Dalit men.**
- **Scale:** official surveys "identified" only ~58,000 manual scavengers (2013–18 — a gross undercount);
  SKA / Bezwada Wilson put it in the **lakhs**.
- **The dodge:** the government declares *"manual scavenging"* (dry latrines) eradicated → therefore *"no
  manual-scavenging deaths"* → while counting sewer/septic deaths separately as *"hazardous cleaning
  accidents."* **June 2025:** Parliament was told *"no death due to manual scavenging"* even as the
  government's own data records **377 sewer deaths (2019–23)** and the NCSK counts **1,313 since 1993** —
  with SKA counting **roughly double.**

## 6 · The per-city picture (our six + Kerala)

- **Kerala** — the national lead (Bandicoot, Genrobotics; "manhole-free" *campaign*, though its own movement
  leaders reject the "ended manual scavenging" claim).
- **Chennai** — strongest of our six (Bandicoot via CMWSSB since 2024) — yet runs **10 of 15 SWM zones**
  through private contractors.
- **Hyderabad** — mechanised desludging fleets (jetting trucks); ~28,000 sanitation workers on contract.
- **Ahmedabad** — a handful of robots (pilot-plus).
- **Mumbai / Bengaluru** — pilot-only.
- **Kolkata** — least mechanised.
- **All six** still kill, because mechanising the public manholes leaves the private tanks to the contract
  worker — and **five of the six are under a standing 2025 Supreme Court order** to end the practice.

## 7 · The test that matters (the Ambedkarite point)

The practice persists not because the technology doesn't exist but because the **caste-assignment** does —
the work is allotted by birth, and the contract form is the modern instrument that keeps that allotment alive
while evading the law. So the real test is **not** *"does the city own a robot?"* It is:

> **Has the city stopped sending one caste into its filth, and given those workers permanent, dignified,
> non-caste-marked municipal jobs?**

By that test, every one of our six cities fails. Mechanisation that simply rebadges the same Valmiki worker
as a "robot operator" on the same precarious contract has not broken caste — it has *re-equipped* it. This is
where Ghosh's corporate-municipality frame reaches its limit (her analysis is property and class) and
**Ambedkar** completes it: the city's one in-house function is held by a hereditary caste of cleaners, hired
by the day, dying in the dark, under a law that has banned their work since 1993 and a state that says they
do not exist.
