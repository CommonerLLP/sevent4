# SevenT4 Mission And Vision

_Status: working project doctrine. Last updated: 2026-06-08._

## Mission

SevenT4 turns India's urban confusion into an accountability map.

It helps residents, organizers, journalists, researchers, and public-spirited
lawyers connect ordinary city problems to the constitutional and administrative
geography that governs them: ward, municipal body, Assembly constituency,
Parliamentary constituency, state department, parastatal, utility, development
authority, finance channel, and public representative.

The practical question is simple:

- Who governs this place?
- Where does the Constitution say urban power should sit?
- Where does the actual decision sit?
- Who must answer in public?

## Vision

SevenT4 should become a public atlas of Indian city-regions. It should show how
the cities that run India's economy and politics are governed, fragmented,
financed, planned, policed, serviced, and neglected.

The atlas should not behave like a neutral smart-city dashboard. It should make
misgovernance legible as a constitutional and social problem. Its purpose is to
move people from private frustration to public accusation, and from public
accusation to collective demands that can be taken to meetings, petitions,
hearings, campaigns, and streets.

## Core Claim

Indian cities are not misgoverned because residents cannot identify problems.
They are misgoverned because responsibility for urban life is fragmented across
municipal corporations, state governments, development authorities,
parastatals, police, utilities, special purpose vehicles, consultants,
contractors, courts, finance bodies, ward councillors, MLAs, MPs, and Union
ministries in ways that protect power from public anger.

SevenT4 exists to make that fragmentation visible, contestable, and politically
usable.

## Ambedkar At The Center

SevenT4 does not read decentralization as inherently democratic. Its starting
point is Ambedkar's question: when power is redistributed, does it become
democratic power, or does it become another administrative form through which
dominant caste and class groups capture the state?

The governing frame is Ambedkarite in five senses.

First, constitutional morality cannot be presumed. Institutions must be designed
and inspected because democratic conduct does not automatically follow from
democratic language.

Second, locality is not innocence. Ambedkar rejected the romance of the village
republic because caste society can turn local self-rule into local tyranny.
SevenT4 applies the same suspicion to municipal and metropolitan governance.
Local scale matters only if it transfers real power to the public and breaks
graded citizenship.

Third, finance is power. In _The Evolution of Provincial Finance in British
India_, Ambedkar showed that colonial decentralization often meant departmental
accounting inside a unitary treasury, not genuine fiscal authority. SevenT4
uses that lesson for urban governance: if money, staff, sanction, data, and
planning remain elsewhere, devolution is grammar without power.

Fourth, democracy is not merely a form of government. It is a mode of associated
life. Urban services must therefore be read through caste, class, gender,
religion, work, migration, settlement status, and spatial segregation. A ward
with heat, sanitation, school, health, transit, or housing deficits is not just
underserved. It may be experiencing graded citizenship in geographic form.

Fifth, administrative detail is political. The atlas treats jurisdiction,
files, departments, budgets, service layers, and representative boundaries as
evidence of how constitutional promises are translated, diluted, or evaded.

Core anchors:

- Constituent Assembly Debates, 4 November 1948: Ambedkar's Draft Constitution
  speech on dual polity, constitutional morality, administrative detail, and
  the village-republic critique.
- BAWS: _The Evolution of Provincial Finance in British India_.
- BAWS: _Annihilation of Caste_ and Ambedkar's wider analysis of social
  democracy, public conscience, and caste as anti-public structure.

## Constitutional Problem

Part IXA of the Constitution, inserted by the Constitution (Seventy-fourth
Amendment) Act, 1992, promises municipalities as institutions of
self-government. Article 243W and the Twelfth Schedule point toward urban
planning, land use, roads, water, sanitation, public health, slum improvement,
poverty alleviation, parks, public amenities, and social justice.

The lived city is usually different. Decision-making is spread across state
departments, municipal corporations, development authorities, transport bodies,
utilities, police, special authorities, finance commissions, Union schemes, and
private or quasi-private implementation vehicles.

SevenT4 asks whether Part IXA has produced democratic urban power or only a
constitutional vocabulary around an older administrative state.

## State-First Urban Power

For ordinary Indian cities, urban government is governed through the States
first. Municipalities exist under state municipal law, and state governments
decide how much power, finance, staff, planning authority, and administrative
control are actually handed to urban local bodies.

That means the main devolution test is not whether MoHUA has a programme or
whether a central scheme names local bodies. The test is whether the State has
transferred real power to the municipality and, through the municipality, to
the people.

MoHUA still matters, but differently. It matters as a source of national urban
missions, model reform language, funding conditions, rankings, data systems,
and policy discourse. It does not normally govern a city the way a State
government, state urban department, development authority, municipal
corporation, utility, or parastatal governs it.

Delhi is the major exception that proves why the atlas needs special models.
Because Delhi is a Union Territory with an elected legislature, Union power,
the Lieutenant Governor, DDA, Delhi Police, Delhi NCT departments, MCD, NDMC,
Delhi Cantonment Board, and NCR institutions all have to be mapped separately.

## Decentralization Spine

SevenT4's research spine treats Indian urban governance as part of a longer
history of decentralization, planning, and state transformation:

1. Colonial administrative decentralization, including the 1907 Royal Commission
   upon Decentralization in India.
2. Nehruvian community development, Etawah, block administration, and imported
   extension-planning models.
3. Balwantrai Mehta's democratic-decentralization doctrine and the Panchayati
   Raj settlement around development administration.
4. Dantwala, block-level planning, district planning, land reform, public
   distribution, and the organization of the poor.
5. Hanumantha Rao and the district as the unit of integrated area planning.
6. Asoka Mehta, Singhvi, and the move from statutory local bodies toward
   constitutional recognition.
7. The 73rd and 74th Amendments as constitutional attempts to repair repeated
   administrative failure.
8. Liberalisation, fiscal decentralization, NGOs, CSR, targeted welfare,
   parastatal growth, and market-facing local government.
9. BRGF and Aspirational Districts as shifts from planning and grants toward
   dashboarded convergence, ranking, competition, and performance federalism.
10. Comparative federalism and local-state studies across the US, Canada,
    Australia, the UK, China, and postcolonial India.

The question running through this spine is not whether power is centralized or
decentralized in the abstract. The question is whether responsibility, finance,
staff, data, sanction, and democratic control move together.

## First Atlas Geography

SevenT4 should organize the national atlas by city-region where the economy,
commute, land market, public services, and administrative authority exceed a
single municipal boundary.

The first working national set should be:

1. Mumbai / MMR
2. Delhi NCR
3. Bengaluru / Greater Bengaluru
4. Hyderabad
5. Chennai
6. Kolkata
7. Pune / Pimpri-Chinchwad
8. Ahmedabad-Gandhinagar-Sanand-Kalol/Kadi-side expansion
9. Surat
10. Jaipur

This is a political-economy atlas set, not a claim that official annual city
GDP rankings are clean or stable. India does not publish a simple authoritative
city-GDP table that maps neatly onto municipal boundaries. If the project needs
a stricter GDP-estimate list, Visakhapatnam should be evaluated against Jaipur.

Ahmedabad is the seed implementation, not the whole political argument. It is a
useful first city because it is relatively legible and relatively well governed
compared with many Indian cities. That makes it a good baseline for showing how
fragmentation persists even where municipal capacity is stronger.

## Urban Agglomeration Rule

SevenT4 must not confuse official city limits with the city people actually
live in. Municipal boundaries are necessary because budgets, wards, councillors,
municipal laws, and service datasets are usually tied to them. But city life is
made through a wider urban agglomeration: commute sheds, labour markets,
migration, rent, land conversion, state infrastructure, police jurisdictions,
schools, hospitals, logistics, caste and class settlement, and gendered
mobility.

The atlas should therefore show official city-limit data while explaining the
larger city-region in which that data operates. Ahmedabad should be explained
through Ahmedabad-Gandhinagar-Sanand-Kalol/Kadi-side expansion and AUDA/GUDA
planning power, not only AMC. Mumbai should be the MMR, not only the Municipal
Corporation of Greater Mumbai. Bengaluru should be read through GBA, the BBMP
transition, BMLTA, BDA, BWSSB, BMRCL, BMTC, BESCOM, and the wider metropolitan
transport and land market.

The operating doctrine is in
[Urban Agglomeration Model](urban-agglomeration-model.md).

## Delhi NCR Rule

Delhi NCR requires special treatment. It should not be flattened into "Delhi"
or treated as a normal municipal corporation.

The NCR atlas must expose at least five accountability ladders:

- Union government, Lieutenant Governor, central land and security powers, DDA,
  Delhi Police, and Union-controlled institutions.
- Delhi NCT's elected government and departments.
- Municipal bodies, including MCD, NDMC, and Delhi Cantonment Board.
- NCR regional planning and cross-state coordination institutions.
- Haryana and Uttar Pradesh urban authorities for Gurugram, Faridabad, Noida,
  Greater Noida, Ghaziabad, and related NCR nodes.

Gurugram belongs inside the NCR atlas as a Haryana sub-atlas. Noida and Greater
Noida belong inside it as Uttar Pradesh sub-atlases. Their value is precisely
that they reveal how a single metropolitan economy is governed through
different state chains, authority-led development models, land regimes,
municipal arrangements, police structures, and fiscal channels.

NCR also has to be explained culturally, economically, and socially. It is a
national-capital society of migration, work, courts, media, universities,
service labour, real estate, industrial corridors, informal settlements, elite
state zones, gendered mobility, and public fear. The 2012 Delhi gang rape and
the protests around it belong in the atlas as a governance question about
transport, policing, lighting, labour, housing, public space, and women's right
to move through the city-region, not as a sensational reference.

## Theory Of Change

SevenT4 changes understanding through a sequence:

1. Start with everyday confusion: potholes, heat, flooding, toilets, schools,
   health posts, buses, parks, libraries, police, housing, waste, ward offices,
   MLAs, MPs, municipal departments, and parastatals.
2. Map the jurisdiction overlap so the user sees the administrative structure
   around the problem.
3. Convert confusion into accusation by naming the public body, representative,
   department, finance channel, or constitutional layer that should answer.
4. Mark devolution gaps where Part IXA says municipal self-government but
   actual authority sits with state agencies, parastatals, SPVs, consultants,
   or distant finance bodies.
5. Make caste and class geography visible so deprivation appears as graded
   citizenship, not neutral scarcity.
6. Produce street-ready claims: the responsible body, the legal or
   constitutional hook, the budget or service evidence, and the public demand.

The output of each ward or city-region page should be a public question people
can use:

Who has the power, who has the money, who has the file, who has the vote, and
why are they not answerable here?

## Product Principles

- Show the city as residents experience it, but connect every layer to an
  institution.
- Treat the State as the primary constitutional gatekeeper for ordinary urban
  devolution, then ask whether power has actually been handed to people through
  local self-government.
- Treat missing data as evidence, not absence.
- Separate legal responsibility, administrative control, fiscal control, and
  representative accountability.
- Preserve source provenance for every layer.
- Avoid technocratic dashboards that admire state capacity without asking who
  it serves.
- Never let caste, class, labour, settlement status, gender, religion, or
  migration disappear behind neutral service metrics.
- Make every screen capable of ending in a public demand.

## Source Spine

The project should keep building around these source families:

- Ambedkar: BAWS, Constituent Assembly Debates, constitutional morality,
  provincial finance, caste and social democracy.
- Constitutional text: Part IXA, Article 243W, Twelfth Schedule, State Municipal
  Acts, metropolitan planning provisions, Finance Commission material.
- Indian official policy and finance: Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs
  and predecessor urban ministries as sources for central schemes, reform
  discourse, standards, data systems, and Delhi/Union Territory exceptions; RBI
  municipal finance, state finance, and urban fiscal material; Planning
  Commission plan documents, working groups, programme guidelines,
  block-planning and district-planning reports; NITI Aayog material where the
  post-Planning Commission state is being analyzed.
- Decentralization committee reports: Royal Commission upon Decentralization,
  Balwantrai Mehta, Dantwala, Hanumantha Rao, Asoka Mehta, Singhvi, and related
  planning reports.
- External development-finance and reform discourse: World Bank / CSDS
  decentralization reports, including the rural decentralization volumes
  archived in the research corpus; IMF fiscal decentralization, public finance,
  urban infrastructure, and governance material where it shaped or interpreted
  Indian reform.
- Anthropology and sociology of the local state: Corbridge, Srivastava,
  Williams, Veron, Nayanika Mathur, Gupta, Sivaramakrishnan, Chakrabarti,
  Purayil, Thakur, and related work.
- Political economy and comparative federalism: Sarkaria, Sinha, Pritchett,
  Saez, and comparative US, Canada, Australia, UK, and China material.
