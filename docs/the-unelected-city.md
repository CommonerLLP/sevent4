# The unelected city: when no one you voted for runs your municipality

The sharpest finding of the SevenT4 atlas is not about a missing library or a slow
bus. It is this: **across the 2020s, most of India's largest cities have spent
years with no elected municipal government at all** — run instead by a
state-appointed IAS administrator. The 74th Amendment promised elected local
self-government. In practice it is suspended, sometimes for over a decade.

This is the power map at its most extreme. The atlas's normal story is *elected
councillor (weak) vs appointed Commissioner (strong)*. In these cities there is no
councillor at all — only the Commissioner. The "form of administration" has wholly
replaced the elected body, exactly the perversion Ambedkar warned the Constituent
Assembly about.

## What it adds up to

At points between 2020 and 2026, **the majority of India's top metros were
simultaneously under unelected administrators** — Mumbai, Pune, Nagpur, Bengaluru,
Jaipur, and (earlier) Chennai. As of mid-2026, **Bengaluru and Jaipur still are.**
Bengaluru's **eleven years** without an elected council — over a city of ~9 million
voters — is the starkest civic-democratic failure among India's major cities.

Two structural patterns produce it:

1. **Reservation / delimitation litigation as a freeze button.** In Maharashtra,
   Karnataka, and Rajasthan, disputes over OBC reservation and ward delimitation
   have parked municipal elections in court for years. Whatever the legal merits,
   the *effect* is that elected local government lapses and state-appointed officers
   govern in its place — indefinitely, with no fixed end.
2. **Restructuring as dissolution.** Bengaluru's Greater Bengaluru Governance Act
   replaced an elected corporation with an appointed authority during a multi-year
   transition — reform that, in the interim, removes the vote entirely.

## The everyday cost: a civic problem with no address

This is not an abstract constitutional point. It is why the pothole stays unfixed.

A pothole, a choked drain, a dead streetlight, a garbage pile, a traffic snarl,
foul air — these are **local, ward-scale problems**, and democracy's mechanism for
fixing them is simple and personal: a resident takes the problem to **their ward
councillor**, who is one person, named, reachable, and whose re-election depends on
whether it gets fixed. The councillor is the *address* a civic problem is sent to.

The unelected city severs that link in two ways, and the atlas documents both:

1. **No councillor at all** (Bengaluru, Jaipur). There is no one to receive the
   complaint and no one whose job depends on resolving it. The problem can only be
   routed to an appointed administrator who answers upward to the state, not
   downward to the street. The resident cannot punish inaction with a vote, because
   there is no election. The feedback loop is dead.
2. **A councillor without power** (the rest — the power-map finding). Even where a
   councillor exists, they can *raise* the issue but not *deliver* the fix — they
   control neither the budget nor the engineering staff, which sit with the
   Standing Committee and the IAS Commissioner. The complaint dissipates up a chain
   the resident did not elect and cannot hold to account.

Either way the citizen cannot do the one thing accountability requires: **match a
specific problem to a specific person who can fix it and whom they can remove.**
That broken match — not a shortage of money or engineers — is why so many Indian
cities cannot address their most basic civic failures. The pothole has no address.

**This is precisely the gap the atlas is built to close.** SevenT4's job is to
re-establish the matching: for any point in the city it names the ward, the
councillor (or, bluntly, the *absence* of one), the MLA, the MP, the Municipal
Commissioner, and which body actually controls the relevant service and budget — so
a resident, an NGO, or a journalist can route a problem to the actor who is actually
responsible, and see exactly where the responsibility was scattered or suspended.
Grievance data (potholes, drains, complaints) layered onto this becomes an
accountability instrument: *here is the problem, here is who is supposed to answer
for it, here is whether you can vote them out.*

## The control group proves it is a choice

**Kerala (Kochi) has never had a gap.** Local-body elections run on time every five
years; the 2025 poll succeeded the 2020 council without interruption. Same
Constitution, same court system, same reservation questions — and yet an
uninterrupted elected local government. The contrast shows the gaps elsewhere are
not an inevitability of Indian law; they are a failure of political will, enabled by
states content to govern cities through their own officers.

## One more pattern, stated plainly

When the delayed polls finally happened, the ruling party swept them: the
**court-forced early-2026 elections returned BJP majorities** in Mumbai, Pune,
Nagpur (Jan 2026) and Ahmedabad (Apr 2026). The atlas records this as fact, without
inferring cause; but the sequence — *years of administrator rule, then a
state-timed, court-forced election won decisively by the party in power at the
state* — is exactly the accountability question the 74th Amendment exists to force,
and exactly what this atlas should make visible.

## Why parties can hold the city: the mayor India never empowered

There is a reason the gaps and the powerlessness persist, and it is comparative.
In **Toronto, Chicago, London, Seattle, New York**, the city is its own political
arena because it has a **directly elected executive mayor** — one person with real
power over the budget, the administration, and (in some) the police, a fixed term, a
personal mandate, and nowhere to hide. A NYC or London mayor who fails on garbage,
transit, crime, or housing loses the next election; and because the office is
powerful, it is also a genuine **counterweight** to the state or national government
(London's mayor against Whitehall on transport; NYC's mayor against the governor).
The city imposes its own accountability, and a party must *perform locally* to hold
it — or lose it, often to the opposition.

India built the opposite by design. The mayor is largely **ceremonial** — frequently
a one-year, indirectly elected post with no executive power — while the real
authority sits with the **state-appointed IAS Municipal Commissioner** and, above
him, the **state government**. The 74th Amendment created elected *councils* but
never created strong elected *executives*; it left the "form of administration" in
the state's hands. The consequence is structural: **a city is not a distinct
accountability arena — it is an extension of the ruling state party, governed through
an appointed officer.** A party can therefore *control* a city without ever facing
the personal, local, executive accountability that a strong-mayor system forces.
Keeping the mayor weak — and, in Bengaluru and Jaipur, keeping the council suspended
altogether — is not a bug for the party in power at the state; it is the mechanism.

This is also why the rare empowered Indian mayor is studied as an anomaly. When
**M.K. Stalin was Mayor of Chennai (1996–2002)** while his father Karunanidhi was
Chief Minister, the office briefly carried real clout ("Singara Chennai") and became
a stepping stone to the Deputy CM's and then CM's chair — but that power flowed from
a *dynastic* link to the state, not from the institution. The empowered Indian mayor
is made by family, not by design; the institution remains deliberately weak. (A
separate literature review on this case is under way against the project's corpus.)

## How the atlas surfaces this

Every city carries a **council-status flag** (elected since DATE / administrator-run
since DATE, with cause). For a ward in Bengaluru or Jaipur, the power-map popup
should say, bluntly: *"No elected councillor — this corporation has had no elected
council since [year]. It is run by [IAS administrator]."* That single line is the
finding.
